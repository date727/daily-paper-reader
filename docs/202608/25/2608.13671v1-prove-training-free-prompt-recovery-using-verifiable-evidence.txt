# PROVE: Training-Free Prompt Recovery using Verifiable Evidence 

# Rupayan Mallick, Mahsa Khoshnoodi, Sarah Adel Bargal 

Department of Computer Science, Georgetown University 

Abstract 

Modern text-to-image models can generate highly realistic im-ages from natural-language prompts, while recent advances in prompt inversion have made it increasingly feasible to recover those prompts from generated outputs, raising new concerns for copyright protection and content ownership. As prompt marketplaces emerge, recovered prompts can enable both the unauthorized reproduction and redistribution of copyrighted creative works, and the exposure of the prompts that encode an artist’s creative recipe in AI-generated content. Existing prompt inversion methods rely on gradient-based optimiza-tion, autoregressive captioning, or reinforcement learning. However, optimization-based methods often produce unread-able prompts, captioning methods hallucinate unverified de-tails, and RL-based approaches frequently overfit to specific generators while introducing evaluation circularity. We in-troduce PROVE (Prompt Recovery with Verified Evidence), a training-free, black-box prompt inversion attack that re-constructs prompts by composing verifiable scene descrip-tions rather than optimizing token sequences, targeting both original copyrighted works and AI-generated content. The resulting prompts are fully auditable, with every recovered claim grounded in explicit image evidence, and are formalized through a precision-constrained recall maximization objec-tive. Across MS-COCO, Flickr30K, and Lexica, using state-of-the-art text-to-image generators, PROVE consistently out-performs optimization, captioning, and RL-based baselines on image similarity (DINO, LPIPS) and text-image alignment (CLIP), without any training, generator access, or fine-tuning, demonstrating a stronger and more practical prompt inversion attack. 

# Introduction 

Text-to-image generation has been transformed by diffusion-and flow-based models such as Stable Diffusion (Esser et al. 2024), DALL-E (Ramesh et al. 2021), FLUX (Labs et al. 2025), and Imagen (Saharia et al. 2022). These models can synthesize highly realistic images from natural-language de-scriptions, with image quality and fidelity largely determined by their ability to interpret user prompts. This capability has democratized image creation, editing, and content genera-tion, enabling non-experts to produce sophisticated visual content through simple textual instructions. However, the same accessibility introduces significant security, safety, and intellectual property concerns. In white-box settings, prompt inversion is typically formu-lated as an optimization problem in either the text-embedding space or the diffusion latent space. While effective at improving image sim-ilarity, optimization-based methods often produce prompts that drift away from natural language, yielding token se-quences that are difficult for humans to interpret. Moreover, the discrete nature of language introduces a fundamental challenge, as textual prompts are inherently incompatible with conventional continuous optimization techniques. In black-box settings, there is no knowledge of model pa-rameters and access is limited to APIs or generated images, prompt inversion is commonly approached through image captioning and vision-language models (VLMs). Methods based on BLIP (Li et al. 2022), CLIP-IG (pharmapsychotic 2023), and VLMs generate prompts directly from visual con-tent by leveraging pretrained vision and language encoders. Although these approaches produce fluent and interpretable descriptions, the prompts aren’t optimized, and often lack mechanisms for verifying whether generated claims are ac-tually supported by the image, reducing prompt fidelity. More recently, PromptStealer (Shen et al. 2024) decom-posed prompt stealing into subject and modifier recovery, using a fine-tuned captioning model to infer the subject and a multi-label detector to predict stylistic modifiers, while PromptMiner (Li et al. 2025b) proposed an RL-based frame-work that mines prompts from online marketplaces and learns to recover them through reinforcement learning, followed by fuzz-based optimization of stylistic modifiers. While promis-ing, this approach relies on the same image generation model for both target and reconstructed images and use the same objective function for the RL optimization and metric compu-tation, raising concerns about generator-specific overfitting and evaluation circularity. We propose a state-of-the-art black-box prompt inver-sion attack, PROVE , that is evidence-based, training-free, and not generator-specific. PROVE consists of four stages: grounded claim proposal using frozen vision-language mod-els and open-vocabulary detection; evidence-gated verifica-tion, where each atomic claim must satisfy a label-anchored CLIP margin on its corresponding image region; geomet-ric fact extraction, which deterministically derives counts and spatial relationships; and deterministic composition, which serializes verified claims into a fluent prompt.We show that PROVE’s generated prompts are SOTA on MS-COCO, Flickr30K, and Lexica (Figure 1), and that the proposed at-tack is robust to reasonable defenses. 

# Related Work 

Prompt inversion for text-to-image models. Prompt in-version recovers a textual description from a target image such that regenerating from that text reproduces the im-age. Two families exist. Soft inversion optimizes continuous pseudo-tokens that are appended to the model vocabulary, as in Textual Inversion (Gal et al. 2022) and multi-concept customization (Kumari et al. 2023); the recovered artifact is an embedding and is not human readable. Hard inver-sion instead returns discrete natural language. PEZ performs gradient-based optimization over discrete tokens to recover prompts that reproduce a target image (Wen et al. 2023). PH2P optimizes interpretable prompts directly from a tar-get image through a delayed projection scheme and L-BFGS (Mahajan et al. 2024) EDITOR initializes from a captioning model, refines in latent space, and decodes back to text (Li et al. 2025a). VGD removes the need for gradients, pairing a language model with CLIP guidance to produce coherent, human-readable prompts (Kim et al. 2025). We additionally evaluate STEPS and PromptMiner (Qiu et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025b). Captioning models such as BLIP-2 are commonly used as a fast approximate baseline for this task (Williams et al. 2024). We note that this line of work is distinct from la-tent inversion methods such as null-text and negative-prompt inversion (Mokady et al. 2023; Miyake et al. 2025), which recover a noise trajectory for editing rather than a textual artifact, therefore falls outside the threat model we consider. All of the hard inversion methods we evaluate take a source image as input and return text. They were developed for prompt engineering, data attribution, model provenance, and watermark validation, and their reported evaluations measure CLIP-space image and text similarity, distributional align-ment between generations from the original and recovered prompts, and human judgments of interpretability (Wen et al. 2023; Mahajan et al. 2024; Williams et al. 2024). We repur-pose them as measurement instruments and evaluate them against a criterion their original benchmarks do not report. 

Inversion as a privacy threat. A smaller body of work al-ready treats inversion adversarially. Prompt stealing attacks such as PromptStealer reconstruct the prompt behind a gen-erated image in order to appropriate its style or content (Shen et al. 2024), and Ye et al. extend this threat across modali-ties with a unified cross-modal inversion approach (Ye et al. 2025). A parallel line of work targets the training data rather than the prompt: membership inference attacks determine whether an image was used in training (Wu et al. 2022), extraction and replication studies show that diffusion mod-els memorize and regurgitate individual training examples (Carlini et al. 2023; Somepalli et al. 2023), and fine-tuning has been shown to amplify this risk (Li et al. 2024). ML-Doctor provides a complementary, holistic risk assessment of inference attacks against machine learning models (Liu et al. 2022). Across this literature the asset at risk is either the prompt or the training set, and the adversary is assumed to hold some privileged relationship to the model. Our threat model assumes neither. 

# Methods 

The task of recovering the prompt used to generate an im-age is referred to in the literature as either prompt inversion 

or prompt stealing ,in both cases the objective is to produce a prompt that reproduces the target image. Existing meth-ods also differ in their assumptions about model access (Li et al. 2025b; Shen et al. 2024), framing the task as either white-box or black-box problem. White-box methods are gradient-based optimization approaches that require access to the model architecture and to the text and vision encoders. Black-box methods, in contrast, assume access only to the target image, with no access to the text-to-image generative model. Our method (Figure 2) is a black-box approach: given only the target image, the objective is to recover the prompt that generated it. In this work, we focus on the black-box setting, with particular emphasis on dense scenes.

# Experiments 

We design experiments to measure image recovery for orig-inal real images and AI-generated images. For the original real images domain, we use the MS-COCO (Lin et al. 2014) and Flickr30K (Plummer et al. 2015) datasets. MS-COCO and Flickr30K provide real photographs paired with human-written captions, and stress prompt recovery on dense, every-day scenes that contain multiple interacting objects, counts, and spatial relations, exactly the regime our per-claim ev-idence pipeline targets. For MS-COCO and Flickr30K a prompt is generated directly from original dataset images. For the generative-art domain, we use the Lexica (Lexica 2024) dataset. Lexica consists of art-style images paired with the engineered prompts that produced them, and stresses recovery of the stylistic and aesthetic modifiers characteristic of prompt marketplaces. For Lexica, following prior work, we use the provided ground-truth prompt to generate an image, which in turn used to generate the inverted prompt. We use two state-of-the-art open-weight text-to-image models both to render recovered prompts back into images and, where noted above, to con-struct the target images: FLUX.1-dev (Labs et al. 2025) and Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large (Esser et al. 2024). Each method is evaluated independently using both generators. Crucially, the generator is used only to render images for evaluation and is applied identically to all methods. We compare against seven prompt-inversion methods drawn from the three established families. Optimization-based: PH2P (Mahajan et al. 2024), STEPS (Qiu et al. 2025), and VGD (Kim et al. 2025), which recover prompts by gradient-based or guided search against a similar-ity objective. Captioning-based: BLIP (Li et al. 2022), CLIP-Interrogator (CLIP-IG) (pharmapsychotic 2023), and a strong VLM-as-expert baseline that prompts a frozen vision-language model to describe the image directly. RL-based: 

PromptMiner (Li et al. 2025b), which learns a prompt-recovery policy via reinforcement learning with fuzz-based modifier optimization. All methods take a single source im-age as input and return text. Since prompt inversion is ulti-mately judged by whether the recovered prompt reproduces the target, we report metrics comparing the resulting image to the target image: DINO (↑), the cosine similarity of self-supervised DINO features; LPIPS (↓), a learned perceptual distance, and CLIP (↑), the CLIP-space cosine similarity be-tween the recovered prompt and the target image (text-image alignment). Reporting a semantic image measure (DINO), a perceptual image measure (LPIPS), and a text-image align-ment measure (CLIP) guards against any single metric being gamed by an off-manifold or degenerate prompt. All gener-ations are done twice using two seeds, and average metrics of the two generations are reported. As shown in Table 1, PROVE achieves the strongest overall image recovery across all three datasets, with qualitative reconstructions in Figure 3 confirming that it recovers object counts and spatial layout more faithfully than the baselines.

# Robustness and Defenses 

Because prompt inversion can be misused, we evaluate PROVE under defenses an image owner might deploy: three post-hoc perturbations from prior work (Shen et al. 2024) such as Noise Injection , Puzzle , and Watermarking , as well as a stronger white-box defense, Adversarial CLIP ,which perturbs the image against the CLIP encoder while leaving it perceptually intact. Results are reported in Table 2. 

Noise Injection adds zero-mean Gaussian noise to the tar-get before inversion, degrading low-level statistics without altering scene content, and thus probes whether inversion depends on precise pixel appearance, a method whose in-formation flows through semantic channels (captioning, de-tection, region description) should be unaffected, whereas one keyed to fine texture should degrade. Puzzle divides the image into a 4 × 4 grid and randomly offsets each patch, disrupting global spatial coherence while leaving local con-tent intact. It perturbs scene composition rather than appear-ance and so tests our geometric-fact channel, where object identity survives within patches, so counts persist, while sup-port and relation predicates, computed from box coordinates, are most exposed to the displacement. Watermarking puts semi-transparent text across the image. Unlike the previous two, it adds content rather than removing it, probing whether verification suppresses inserted content lacking a grounded visual reference. Following the white-box formulation of (Shen et al. 2024), 

Adversarial CLIP applies iterative FGSM to the CLIP im-age encoder, minimizing cosine similarity between perturbed and clean embeddings within an L∞ ball ( ϵ = 8 /255 , 100 steps) – the standard bound in the adversarial-robustness lit-erature, at which the perturbation is imperceptible in natural images. # Conclusion 

We propose PROVE, a training-free, optimization-free prompt inversion (prompt stealing) method that recovers the prompt used to generate a target image. PROVE proceeds in four stages. First, a frozen VLM and open-vocabulary detection propose fine-grained atomic claims about the im-age. Second, each claim is retained only if it satisfies a label-anchored CLIP margin on its corresponding region. Third, counts and spatial relations are derived deterministi-cally from the detections rather than asserted by the VLM. Finally, the verified claims are serialized into a single flu-ent prompt, with no generator in the loop. PROVE recovers more descriptive and accurate prompts than the considered baselines. These gains come from verification and determin-istic derivation, without any training, optimization, or ac-cess to the target generator. While this constitutes a stronger prompt-stealing attack, with direct implications for copyright and the reproduction of content, our pipeline verifies visual content while, by construction, refusing artist and platform attribution, separating faithful description from unauthorized reproduction of an artist’s identity. PROVE reports visual ev-idence without asserting authorship. We hope this separation of description from identity offers a useful reference point for studying copyright concerns. References

# Implementation Details 

Models. All components are frozen, off-the-shelf mod-els; PROVE requires no training or gradient optimization. We use Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct as the vision-language model (VLM) gϕ for global captioning, scene-attribute ex-traction, object inventory, and per-region slot-schema de-scription. Open-vocabulary detection Dθ uses OWLv2 (ViT-B/16, ensemble), and the evidence gate E scores claims with CLIP ViT-L/14. Region proposals are deduplicated with non-maximum suppression at an IoU threshold of 0.5.

Pipeline configuration. Detection runs on a 512 × 512 

working copy, while all region crops are taken at native res-olution to preserve fine detail; crops are padded by a factor 

p = 0 .2 of the box dimensions. The evidence margin is 

δ = 0 .03 : a claim c over region xi with label ℓi is retained if and only if f (xi, c ) ≥ f (xi, ℓ i) − δ, i.e., it must match the crop at least as well as the bare object label up to the margin. Claims failing the gate fall back to their object label, and multi-clause claims are salvaged clause-by-clause before rejection. Geometric facts (counts, support, and spatial rela-tions) are derived deterministically from detection boxes and bypass the evidence gate. We cap each image at Mp person instances and Mo object regions. 

Generation and evaluation. Recovered prompts are ren-dered with FLUX.1-dev and SD3.5-Large under identical sampling settings ( T inference steps, guidance scale g, fixed seeds {s1, s 2}) for all methods, so differences reflect the prompt rather than the generator. We evaluate 100 images each from MS-COCO, Flickr30K, and Lexica. 

Hardware. All experiments run on a single NVIDIA L40S GPU. As PROVE is inference-only, a full inversion requires one detector pass, one CLIP pass per claim, and O(regions )
