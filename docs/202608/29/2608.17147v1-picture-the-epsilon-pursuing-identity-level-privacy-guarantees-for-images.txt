# Picture the Epsilon: Pursuing Identity-Level Privacy Guarantees for Images 

Arman Zareian Jahromi ∗, Vishnu Bondalakunta ∗, Mohammad Akbar Bin Shah ∗,Naimul Haque †, Shuangqing Wei †, George T. Amariucai ∗∗Kansas State University, Manhattan, KS, USA {zareian, vishnub, mohammadakbar, amariucai }@ksu.edu

Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, LA, USA {naimul.haque, swei }@lsu.edu 

Abstract —Image-to-image face generators are widely used, and visual dissimilarity between their outputs and source images is sometimes treated as evidence of privacy. Auditing whether these systems satisfy formal identity-level (ε, δ )-differential pri-vacy requires choosing among several distinct routes for con-verting embedding-space observations into estimates or bounds on the differential privacy parameter ε. We present a compara-tive study of four such audits applicable to pre-trained, black-box face generators: a Gaussian-mechanism reading of per-identity sensitivity (G AUSS MECH ); a per-dimension kernel-density log-ratio aggregated by basic composition (KDE-LR); an analytical population-level lower bound on pure-DP ε derived from the maximum mean discrepancy via the total variation distance (MMD-TV); and a hypothesis-testing evaluation of a cross-validated classifier’s out-of-fold ROC (ROC-HT). For each method we make explicit its assumptions, hyperparameter dependence, finite-sample limitations, and the regime in which its ε estimate is informative. Applied to Face-Fusion and InstantID across multiple identity encoders and reference datasets, the audits consistently reveal substantial identity distinguishability while reporting markedly different 

ε estimates that reflect each method’s distinct assumptions and finite-sample treatment. In this high-distinguishability regime, the experiments do not support a reliable ranking of the four methods. Their relative trade-offs should be evaluated on partially private mechanisms, which we identify as the natural next study. The resulting framework places these audits in a shared identity-level audit setting and clarifies how their assumptions and finite-sample treatments shape the resulting differential privacy estimates. 

Index Terms —Differential privacy, privacy auditing, face recog-nition, maximum mean discrepancy, kernel density estimation, hypothesis testing, image-to-image generation. 

## 1. Introduction 

Image-to-image face generators are now part of con-sumer and prosumer pipelines: face-swap systems such as FaceFusion [1] replace an identity in a video with an-other, and diffusion-based zero-shot identity models such as InstantID [2] synthesize a target person in new poses or scenes from a few reference images. These systems are not advertised as privacy-preserving; they are explicitly identity-transferring or identity-preserving by design. Nevertheless, visual dissimilarity between an input frame and the gen-erated output is sometimes treated as informal evidence of privacy, and as identity-aware image generation gets used in adjacent pipelines (face anonymization, deepfake mitigation, privacy-aware data augmentation) the question of how much identity actually survives the generator becomes a measurement question. We use FaceFusion and InstantID as high-distinguishability stress tests. InstantID is designed to preserve its reference identity; in our FaceFusion setup, one donor is fixed while the target images vary, allowing us to test whether the supposedly replaced target identity remains detectable in the output. Differential privacy [3], [4] provides a precise ratio con-straint for such a measurement: a randomized mechanism 

M is (ε, δ )-DP if its output distributions obey the required inequality for every adjacent pair of private inputs. Here the private input is an identity label rather than a database. We use complete identity adjacency : every two distinct identities must satisfy the DP inequalities, and each identity indexes a per-query output distribution. Mathematically, this is a local-DP condition on the identity-label domain because the channel acts directly on one private label. We call it dis-tributional because sampling an image from that identity’s reference distribution is part of the channel’s randomness. This secret-indexed view is related to Pufferfish privacy [5]. Several routes exist for turning an embedding-space ob-servation into an estimate or lower bound on (ε, δ ), and the literature does not converge on a single one. A sensitivity-and-Gaussian-mechanism reading treats the difference be-tween per-identity means as the input change and reads off a closed-form score. This borrows the algebra used by calibrated feature- and image-space mechanisms [6], [7], but its retrospective application to arbitrary generated outputs is a diagnostic rather than a formal lower bound. A per-dimension kernel-density log-ratio similarly gives a marginal diagnostic whose coordinate sum is not a joint-mechanism guarantee. The hypothesis-testing characteriza-tion of differential privacy [8], [9] translates the popula-tion operating point of a fixed distinguisher into a lower bound on ε and underlies empirical DP auditing [10]– [12]. Prior work also connects maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) [13] to DP auditing [14]–[16]. We study a pure-DP chain through total variation [8], [17], [18], compare it with the tighter Hockey-Stick-based result of Gonz´ alez et al. [16], and evaluate all four routes on a shared task and arXiv:2608.17147v1 [cs.CR] 17 Aug 2026 data. This paper presents that comparison for pre-trained, black-box image-to-image face generators. The four meth-ods we study are:  

> •

GAUSS MECH (Section 3.1): a Gaussian-mechanism-inspired diagnostic from the L2 distance between per-identity means and a pooled residual scale;  

> •

KDE-LR (Section 3.2): a basic-composition sum of per-dimension kernel-density log-ratios, with a smoothing weight α; 

> •

MMD-TV (Section 3.3): an analytical lower bound on pure-DP ε derived from the maximum mean discrepancy through the chain MMD ≤ 2 dTV and the TV characterization of pure DP;  

> •

ROC-HT (Section 3.4): a hypothesis-testing eval-uation based on the out-of-fold receiver operating characteristic of a cross-validated classifier. For each method we make explicit its assumptions, its hyperparameter dependence, and the regime in which its 

ε estimate or lower bound is informative. We then evaluate all four on the same audit task across two identity encoders (ArcFace, FaceNet), two reference datasets (VGGFace2, CelebA), and two generators (FaceFusion, InstantID), for a total of eight conditions. 

Contributions.  

> •

We present a comparative framework that places GAUSS MECH , KDE-LR, MMD-TV, and ROC-HT side by side on the same identity-substitution audit task with a shared per-condition pair list and a common 100-pair summary, and clarifies for each method which quantities are formal ε lower bounds and which are finite-sample diagnostics.  

> •

We document the pure-DP MMD-TV specialization obtained by composing MMD ≤ 2 dTV [17] with the total variation characterization of pure DP [8], [18], state its population and finite-sample limits, and compare it explicitly with the tighter Hockey-Stick-based MMD result used in prior auditing work [15], [16].  

> •

We add a finite-sample ROC-HT protocol that sep-arates pair and threshold selection from evalua-tion: all adaptive choices use a development split, while fixed classifiers and thresholds for 40 selected, vertex-disjoint identity pairs are evaluated once on untouched data with simultaneous one-sided exact confidence bounds.  

> •

We empirically evaluate the four methods on eight (encoder × dataset × generator) conditions of Face-Fusion and InstantID auditing. The four audits con-sistently reveal substantial identity distinguishabil-ity; the reserved-test protocol certifies positive ε

lower bounds for all 20 selected VGGFace2 pairs, with 15 exceeding ε = 1, while its loss of power on CelebA exposes the sample cost of finite-sample certification. The methods’ different scales still do not support a reliable ranking in this high-distinguishability regime. The honest finding is that the four methods cannot be ranked on these generators because the audited regime is already highly distinguishable: every method reports sub-stantial identity signal, but the reported numbers are con-trolled by method-specific assumptions, endpoint behavior, or looseness rather than by a shared notion of tightness. None of the evaluated off-the-shelf generators combines a known identity-level guarantee under our threat model with tunable, intermediate distinguishability. We therefore outline in Section 5 a controlled calibrated-noise benchmark on which the four methods could produce non-endpoint, comparable ε values.

2.3. Face Identity Encoders and Generators 

A face identity encoder is a function ϕ : I →

Rd mapping an image x ∈ I to an identity em-bedding. We use precomputed 512-dimensional Arc-Face/InsightFace embeddings [19], [20] and precomputed 512-dimensional InceptionResnet-V1 embeddings produced with facenet-pytorch [21]. The repository does not preserve the exact encoder checkpoint identifiers, so we do not infer a training set or checkpoint variant from the arrays alone. Before every audit, each embedding is L2-normalized to the unit hypersphere. We treat ϕ as a fixed identity-oriented representation, not as ground truth; encoder errors propagate into all four audits (Section 8). We audit two generators from distinct architectural fam-ilies. FaceFusion [1] is an open-source face-swap system, and InstantID [2] is a diffusion-based zero-shot identity generator. In every FaceFusion condition, a single donor is held fixed and the images associated with each dataset identity are supplied as targets; the stored identity label therefore denotes the target identity. InstantID conditions group outputs by the reference identity used for gener-ation. The official InstantID pipeline uses InsightFace’s 

antelopev2 identity model; if the precomputed outputs followed that default, the ArcFace/InsightFace audit partially overlaps the representation used to condition generation. The absent generation manifest prevents us from verifying that configuration, and the FaceNet conditions provide the cleaner cross-encoder check. We use both systems as high-distinguishability stress tests rather than privacy mecha-nisms.

2.5. Threat Model 

Distributional mechanism. Let Pid be the reference-dataset distribution of images labeled with identity id , and let R

collect any internal randomness of the generator. For In-stantID, X ∼ Pid is a reference image and the generated image is Y = gID (X; R). For FaceFusion, X ∼ Pid is a target image, one donor image d0 is fixed for every identity, and the generated image is Y = gFF (X; d0, R ). The pre-encoder image-output channel and the embedding channel actually audited are 

Mimg (id) = Law( Y | X ∼ Pid , R ∼ PR) ,

Mϕ(id) = Law( ϕ(Y ) | X ∼ Pid , R ∼ PR) . (8) Thus identity-conditioned image sampling is part of the channel being measured. Each CelebA identity contributes 26–35 usable generated embeddings, whereas each VG-GFace2 identity contributes 90–100. Operationally, the channel models one query whose image is drawn from the stated identity-conditioned population. It does not guarantee privacy for an arbitrary fixed photograph selected by a user, because that deployment conditions on one particular image rather than averaging over X ∼ Pid . This secret-indexed family resembles the Pufferfish organization of secrets and data-generating distributions [5], while Definition 1 imposes the ordinary pairwise DP inequalities. For InstantID, the private label is the reference identity. For FaceFusion, it is the target identity; the donor d0 is identical for both classes in every pairwise test. The classifier receives one generated 512-dimensional embedding and predicts which of two target identities produced it, so target separation cannot be caused by changing the donor. 

Adjacency and post-processing. We use complete adja-cency over identity labels: any two distinct labels id A and 

id B are adjacent. This is the DP convention specifying which input pairs must satisfy the inequality, not a claim that the identities are similar. Because Mϕ = ϕ ◦ M img 

and the encoder is deterministic, any DP guarantee for the generated-image distribution Mimg would also hold after encoding. Conversely, a confidence-certified violation for 

Mϕ refutes the same proposed guarantee for Mimg . Passing an encoder audit does not prove privacy of the generated image. 

Definition 1 (Identity-level (ε, δ )-DP) . The distributional channel Mϕ is identity-level (ε, δ )-DP if, for every ordered pair of distinct identities (id A, id B ) and every measurable 

S ⊆ Rd,

Pr[ Mϕ(id A) ∈ S] ≤ eε Pr[ Mϕ(id B ) ∈ S] + δ. 

This is equivalent to requiring that no test of a single mechanism output can distinguish Mϕ(id A) from Mϕ(id B )

better than the bound in Equation 2. 

Adversary. The adversary observes one draw from Mϕ and tests which identity distribution produced it. The precom-puted outputs provide repeated observations used to estimate this single-draw channel; the paper makes no group-privacy claim for releasing the full batch.

3.4. ROC-HT: Hypothesis-Testing Bound from a Classifier’s ROC 

ROC-HT applies the hypothesis-testing characterization of differential privacy [8], [9] to a classifier trained to dis-tinguish two identity distributions. At the population level, a fixed distinguisher gives a lower-bound route. We use pooled out-of-fold ROC curves for the broad comparative diagnos-tic, then run a separate development-selected, reserved-test protocol for finite-sample certification.

Pros.  

> •

Distribution-free at the population level. Equation 15 follows the Kairouz–Oh–Viswanath characterization of pure DP at δ = 0 . For a fixed population dis-tinguisher, it requires no parametric model for the output distribution.  

> •

Improves with the distinguisher. A stronger fixed population test can yield a larger lower bound.  

> •

Cross-validation reduces training-set optimism. Out-of-fold operating points are not evaluated on the same examples used to train each fold’s classifier. This reduces training-set optimism, but it is not a substitute for a finite-sample confidence certificate. 

Cons.  

> •

Diverges at ROC endpoints. The raw empirical OOF value is +∞ whenever the ROC contains an oper-ating point with [TPR = 1 and [FPR < 1, or with 

[FPR = 0 and [TPR > 0. This endpoint divergence is algebraically correct for Equation 15, but it does not by itself imply exact zero-error separation. We therefore report AUC and separately track the stricter exact corner ([FPR , [TPR) = (0 , 1) . 

> •

Sensitive to classifier choice. The bound is only as tight as the trained classifier. A random forest in place of logistic regression returns a different operating point on the same data, which is why we report classifier sensitivity as a diagnostic rather than treating one classifier as canonical.  

> •

The out-of-fold value is not a certificate. Five fold-specific classifiers produce the pooled scores, and the maximizing threshold is selected on those same out-of-fold predictions. We therefore keep this quantity as a diagnostic and use a separate reserved-test pro-tocol with exact simultaneous confidence treatment in Section 4.3.  

> •

Sample-size dependence in the endpoint regime. 

The OOF value +∞ is an empirical finite-sample statement about the observed ROC. A larger audit could reveal overlap and move the relevant operating points away from the zero-denominator endpoints. 

Hyperparameters. ROC-HT has three: (i) the classifier family (we use logistic regression and include a random-forest sensitivity check in Section 4.4); (ii) the number of cross-validation folds (we use 5); (iii) the per-pair sample size, which is fixed by the data ( n ≈ 100 on VGGFace2, 

n ≈ 30 on CelebA). The first is the principal lever for tight-ening the bound; the second has the conventional choice; the third is determined by the reference dataset.

4.3. Finite-Sample ROC Certificates on Reserved Test Data 

The out-of-fold ROC summaries above are broad diag-nostics, not confidence certificates. We therefore run a sepa-rate protocol that makes all adaptive choices before touching the evaluation data. Within each condition, every identity’s samples are split once into 70% development and 30% test subsets. On development data only, we score the com-mon first 100 pairs with five-fold out-of-fold predictions, greedily select the five highest-ranked pairs subject to no identity appearing twice within a condition, and choose one threshold for each pair. 4.5. Honest Comparison 

The methods cannot be ranked from these experiments. The classifier and MMD tests provide direct empirical evi-dence of identity distinguishability, the reserved-test ROC protocol certifies all 20 selected VGGFace2 pairs under one simultaneous confidence family, and the two modeling diagnostics are consistent with the same qualitative con-clusion. Their numerical scales differ for method-specific reasons and do not reveal relative tightness at intermediate privacy levels. ## 5. Discussion 

5.1. Why the Four Methods Cannot Be Ranked Here 

Not every number in Table 1 has the same formal status. MMD-TV is a population pure-DP lower-bound route evaluated here with an uncertified sample estimate; ROC-HT is a hypothesis-testing route whose broad OOF estimate diverges at ROC endpoints, while Table 2 gives separately certified selected-pair bounds; G AUSS MECH and KDE-LR are diagnostics tied to modeling and aggregation assumptions. A natural question is still which audit is most informative. On the eight conditions of Table 1, that ques-tion is not well-posed. The generators are already highly distinguishable, and the reported values are dominated by each method’s assumptions, confidence treatment, or end-point behavior: KDE-LR reports values in the thousands, GAUSS MECH in the tens, MMD-TV below one, and ROC-HT has high AUC with raw endpoint-divergent OOF values. The four methods therefore do not separate cleanly on these generators. 

5.2. A Benchmark on Which the Four Methods Could Differ 

The cleanest comparison would use a per-query bench-mark with a known privacy guarantee and a controllable range of distinguishability. One construction is to add cal-ibrated isotropic Gaussian noise to the normalized encoder output: 

fMν (id) = ϕ(g(X)) + ξ, X ∼ Pid ,ξ ∼ N (0 , ν 2Id).

where Pid is the identity’s source-image distribution and 

ν is varied. Because L2 normalization places every output on the unit sphere Sd−1, the L2 distance between any two outputs is at most 2 (the diameter of Sd−1). The per-pair L2 sensitivity of the mean-shift mechanism this benchmark is calibrated against therefore satisfies ∆2 ≤ 2, so the analytic Gaussian-mechanism calibration of Balle and Wang [30] at a fixed nonzero δ supplies a reference upper guarantee. The audits could then be compared against that reference as ν

increases and the ROC leaves its endpoint regime. This ex-periment would evaluate calibration and sensitivity, not turn the original black-box generators into certified mechanisms. Such a controlled benchmark is needed because none of the evaluated off-the-shelf generators provides both a known guarantee for our identity channel and tunable intermediate distinguishability. 

5.3. Which Method to Use When 

The four methods are not interchangeable. G AUSS MECH 

is a cheap diagnostic for mechanisms plausibly dominated by additive Gaussian noise. KDE-LR exposes coordinate-level marginal differences but its sum is not a joint DP guarantee. MMD-TV supplies a classifier-free population lower-bound route, provided its sample estimate receives a one-sided finite-sample treatment. ROC-HT can exploit a strong distinguisher; our reserved-test protocol shows how a fixed, independently evaluated test and simultaneous confidence correction turn that route into a finite-sample certificate, at the cost of reduced power on small test sets. These requirements, rather than the magnitudes in Table 1, should determine the audit choice. 

## 6. Related Work 

DP auditing. Jagielski, Ullman, and Oprea [10] audit DP-SGD using membership-inference attacks; Nasr et al. [11] instantiate strong canary adversaries; and Steinke, Nasr, and Jagielski [12] develop single-run auditing. These works use hypothesis tests to lower-bound effective privacy parame-ters. Their neighborhoods and mechanisms differ from ours: they audit training algorithms under record-level changes, whereas we study an inference-time distribution indexed by identity labels. The hypothesis-testing conversion is not our contribution; our focus is its comparison with three other embedding-space routes in this identity-level setting. 

Training-data privacy in generative models. Carlini et al. [31] demonstrate extraction of memorized diffusion-model training examples, while Hu and Pang [32] study membership inference against denoising diffusion models. Those works ask whether training records are memorized or identifiable. We instead ask whether a generated-output dis-tribution is distinguishable across two input identity labels. The threat models are complementary and neither result implies the other. 

DP for image and generative mechanisms. DP-SGD pro-tects a training algorithm under a specified dataset neigh-borhood [33], whereas feature-space and local-DP image mechanisms add calibrated noise to an image or represen-tation before subsequent processing [6], [7]. These guar-antees answer different questions from our distributional inference-time audit. Our G AUSS MECH diagnostic borrows the algebra of Gaussian calibration, but neither it nor KDE-LR inherits a guarantee from those prior mechanisms when applied retrospectively to arbitrary black-box outputs. 

MMD, total variation, and the hypothesis-testing charac-terization of DP. The kernel two-sample formulation used here follows Gretton et al. [13]. Sriperumbudur et al. [17] studied its relationship to other integral probability metrics, including the comparison underlying Lemma 1. The TV and hypothesis-testing characterizations of DP follow Kairouz, Oh, and Viswanath [8], Ghazi and Issa [18], and the f -DP formulation of Dong, Roth, and Su [9]. Kernel-based DP auditors include Domingo-Enrich and Mroueh [14], DP-Auditorium [15], and the sequential MMD audit of Gonz´ alez et al. [16]. The mathematical pieces of MMD-TV are therefore pre-existing; our contribution is their explicit pure-DP specialization and their assumption-matched empirical comparison beside three other identity-level audit routes. 

## 7. Ethics and Dual Use 

This work measures identity leakage in face-generation systems and therefore involves sensitive biometric data and dual-use risk. Its intended use is defensive evaluation of privacy claims. The same methods could support identity recognition, so we report aggregate embedding-level results and do not release generated faces or identity-specific attack artifacts. The study uses selected subsets of public face datasets and precomputed outputs; it collects no new par-ticipant data, conducts no user study, and does not attempt to re-identify unnamed individuals. Public availability does not remove concerns about consent, demographic bias, or downstream misuse. Encoder performance may vary across demographic groups, but the available metadata do not support a subgroup analysis; this limits the fairness con-clusions that can be drawn. Artifact releases should exclude face images, minimize identity-linked metadata, document dataset licenses and model terms, and state the intended defensive use. 

## 8. Limitations and Future Work 

Generators, data, and provenance. The comparison is limited to precomputed FaceFusion and InstantID out-puts built from selected VGGFace2 and CelebA iden-tities. The loader removes missing and zero-norm vec-tors; after filtering, the ArcFace+CelebA+FaceFusion and FaceNet+CelebA+FaceFusion conditions each retain one du-plicated vector. The FaceFusion protocol fixes one donor and varies target images by dataset identity, so the stored labels represent target identities. However, the repository lacks a versioned generation manifest containing exact revisions, prompts, seeds, checkpoints, and the donor asset identifier. We therefore cannot reproduce image generation from this repository alone or attribute differences to a specific gen-erator revision. Other generators, data distributions, donors, or preprocessing choices may produce different results. 

Encoder as the identity oracle. The four audits treat the identity encoder ϕ as a fixed proxy for identity. A generator that leaks identity as either encoder defines it but not as a human defines it would still register strong distinguishability under our audits. We probe encoder de-pendence with ArcFace/InsightFace and FaceNet representa-tions (Section 2.3); the qualitative verdict is preserved across both, although the numerical values shift. Exact checkpoint identifiers for the precomputed embeddings are unavailable. In addition, InstantID’s official pipeline uses the ArcFace-family antelopev2 representation for conditioning, so the ArcFace+InstantID result may benefit from representa-tion overlap; FaceNet+InstantID is the cleaner cross-encoder check. 

High-distinguishability regime. The principal limitation of this paper is that the audited generators are already highly distinguishable (Section 4.5). The comparison is honest about this: it does not allow us to claim that one method is tighter than another at intermediate privacy levels. The natu-ral follow-up is the calibrated-noise mechanism sketched in Section 5, on which the four methods would produce non-endpoint, comparable ε values across a sweep of the noise scale. Per-query versus multi-query privacy. Definition 1 is a per-query identity-level notion: the mechanism output is one generated embedding, and the N images per identity are used to estimate the corresponding per-identity output distribution. We do not claim a group-privacy guarantee for releasing the full generated batch from an identity. An adversary who observes many independent outputs from the same identity can compose evidence across queries, and translating the per-query audit numbers to a multi-query release requires specifying the composition model and the number of queries. We therefore avoid dividing or multiplying the reported ε values by N in the main comparison. 

Finite-sample certification. The population MMD and hypothesis-testing inequalities are valid, but the reported MMD-TV and broad OOF ROC sample estimates are not confidence-certified lower bounds. Section 4.3 adds a sep-arate reserved-test ROC evaluation with simultaneous exact confidence treatment for five development-selected, vertex-disjoint pairs per condition. Its guarantees are conditional on iid sampling from the defined Pid , concern adaptively selected stress-test pairs rather than a representative pair sample, and lose all power on the smaller CelebA test sets under the global 40-pair correction. A one-sided MMD concentration or sequential treatment remains future work. 

Neighborhood and deployment choices. Definition 1 treats identity-conditioned image sampling from Pid as mechanism randomness. It does not establish DP for a fixed user-selected photograph or for releasing multiple outputs. In the FaceFusion audit the donor is fixed, so the reported values do not characterize privacy under a changing-donor neigh-borhood. Per-image, prompt, variable-donor, and dataset-level neighborhoods require new mechanism definitions and, in some cases, different sampling and sensitivity analyses; the current numerical results do not transfer automatically. 

Representation dependence. Every conclusion is condi-tional on the selected encoders and preprocessing. Using two encoders is a robustness check, not proof that all identity information in the pixels is captured. Extension to another modality would require a new threat model, representation validation, and finite-sample study rather than a direct reuse of the reported numbers. 

## 9. Conclusion 

We presented a comparative study of four empirical differential privacy audits applicable to pre-trained, black-box image-to-image face generators: G AUSS MECH , KDE-LR, MMD-TV, and ROC-HT. For each audit we made explicit its definition, assumptions, hyperparameter depen-dence, finite-sample status, and route from an embedding-space measurement to an ε diagnostic or population lower bound. We documented the MMD-TV pure-DP special-ization for positive-definite kernels satisfying k(x, x ) ≤ 1,aligned the empirical comparison on common identity pairs, and added a development-selected, reserved-test ROC-HT certificate with simultaneous exact confidence control. Across the eight encoder × dataset × generator con-ditions, the classifier and two-sample tests reveal substan-tial identity-distinguishing signal. Our finite-sample ROC protocol (Section 4.3) sets aside an untouched test split per identity: pair selection, classifier fitting, and threshold selection use only the development split, and the test split is reserved for one fixed evaluation. Evaluated on this reserved test split, the protocol certifies positive selected-pair ε lower bounds for all 20 VGGFace2 stress-test pairs under one simultaneous 95% confidence family; 15 exceed ε = 1 .Its inability to certify the much smaller CelebA test sets despite high AUC makes the sample cost of finite-sample DP auditing explicit. The modeling diagnostics are consistent with the distinguishability conclusion, but their numerical values differ because of assumptions, aggregation, finite-sample treatment, and ROC endpoint behavior, not because the experiments identify a tightest audit. G AUSS MECH and KDE-LR are not formal joint-mechanism lower bounds here; the MMD-TV and broad out-of-fold ROC-HT values remain uncertified sample estimates, even though the ROC-HT values produced by the separate reserved-test protocol of Section 4.3 are certified. A benchmark with a known privacy guarantee whose audited ROC keeps every operating point strictly inside the unit square, so that no point has
