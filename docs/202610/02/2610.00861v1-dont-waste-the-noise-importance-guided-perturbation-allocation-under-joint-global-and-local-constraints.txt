# Don’t Waste the Noise: Importance-Guided Perturbation Allocation under Joint Global and Local Constraints 

Melika Shirian 1∗, Kianoosh Vadaei 1∗

1Independent Researcher melika.shi@mehr.ui.ac.ir, k.vadaei@mehr.ui.ac.ir 

Abstract 

Adversarial optimization under a shared ℓ1 budget requires de-ciding not only how much perturbation to use, but also where that limited budget should be spent. This allocation problem becomes particularly important when individual input coordi-nates are subject to local magnitude constraints, which restrict the extent to which perturbation can be concentrated on a small number of locations. We introduce an importance-guided al-location mechanism that uses a fixed clean-gradient prior to steer perturbation toward model-sensitive regions while leav-ing the feasible perturbation set unchanged. A centered al-location objective encourages perturbation at above-average importance locations and discourages unnecessary expendi-ture elsewhere, thereby redistributing rather than enlarging the available budget. Across ten robust model–dataset configura-tions under a common capacity-limited threat setting, the pro-posed method improves attack success over matched APGD-and PMA-based baselines by 2.52 to 17 .70 percentage points. Allocation analysis shows that these gains are accompanied by substantially greater perturbation mass in high-importance regions without increased global ℓ1 consumption. Mechanism ablations further show that centered non-uniform redistribu-tion provides part of the benefit, while model-derived impor-tance yields an additional improvement. These results identify perturbation allocation as a distinct and practically relevant dimension of adversarial optimization under shared-budget, locally constrained threat models. 

Introduction 

Adversarial attacks under ℓ1-bounded threat models have be-come increasingly effective at identifying vulnerabilities in image classifiers. Unlike an ℓ∞ constraint, which indepen-dently limits every input coordinate, an ℓ1 constraint pro-vides a shared perturbation budget that may be distributed unevenly across the input (Croce and Hein 2023; Pintor et al. 2021). Specialized methods such as ℓ1-APGD exploit this geometry by concentrating perturbation on influential coor-dinates (Croce and Hein 2023), while sparse attacks sim-ilarly exploit small subsets of sensitive input components (Modas, Moosavi-Dezfooli, and Frossard 2019a; Croce and Hein 2019). We consider a shared global mean-ℓ1 budget together with coordinate-wise magnitude capacities and valid image-range constraints. The global constraint limits the total available perturbation, while the local capacities limit how much of that budget may be concentrated at any individual coordinate (Croce and Hein 2019). When a useful coordinate reaches its local capacity, the optimizer must distribute the remaining budget across other feasible coordinates. This creates a budget-allocation problem that is related to, but distinct from, sparse optimization. Sparse methods primarily determine which coordinates should receive large changes, whereas capacity-limited optimization must also determine how a shared budget should be distributed when individual coordinates cannot absorb arbitrarily large per-turbations. The formal feasible set is defined in the Method section. All methods in our main evaluation operate under the same capacity-limited feasible set. Separately parameterized conventional and capacity-limited ℓ1 results are retained as descriptive comparisons in Appendix M; because their global budget parameterizations differ, they are not used to isolate the causal effect of local capacities. 

Importance-Guided Budget Allocation 

We address the allocation problem using a fixed, model-derived importance prior. Given a clean input, we construct a spatial importance map from the model gradient (Simonyan, Vedaldi, and Zisserman 2014), normalize it, and center it around its spatial mean. The resulting allocation reward is added to the adversarial objective to encourage perturbation magnitude to shift toward above-average importance loca-tions. Crucially, the importance prior does not change the threat model. It does not mask low-importance coordinates, enlarge local capacities, or provide additional perturbation budget. Instead, it changes only the optimization preference within the same feasible region. This distinguishes our approach from attacks that explicitly select a sparse support or restrict perturbations to salient regions (Papernot et al. 2015a; Dong et al. 2020b). Centering is important because it prevents the allocation term from simply rewarding larger perturbations everywhere. Instead, it creates a relative preference that encourages per-turbation mass to move from below-average toward above-average importance locations. The full importance construc-tion, allocation objective, and optimization procedure are defined in the Method section. 

Main Findings and Contributions 

We evaluate the proposed mechanism across ten robust model–dataset configurations spanning CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet. Under the common capacity-limited threat model, our method improves attack success over the highest-ASR matched APGD/PMA baseline in every config-uration, with gains of 2.52 –17 .70 percentage points. These improvements are accompanied by a systematic change in budget allocation. Matched baselines place approx-imately 10 .6–11 .3% of their perturbation mass in the highest-importance 10% of spatial locations, whereas our method places 19 .2–30 .4% there while remaining within the same global mean-ℓ1 budget. Mechanism ablations further sepa-rate two effects: centered non-uniform redistribution itself improves optimization, while alignment with model-derived importance provides an additional gain. Our main contributions are: 

• Perturbation allocation as an optimization problem. 

We study how a shared global mean-ℓ1 budget should be distributed when individual coordinates are subject to lo-cal magnitude capacities, explicitly separating feasibility from allocation preference. 

• Centered importance-guided allocation. We introduce a soft, model-derived allocation objective that guides per-turbation toward more informative regions without mask-ing coordinates or changing the feasible perturbation set. 

• Mechanistic empirical evidence. Across ten robust model–dataset configurations, the mechanism improves matched APGD/PMA-style baselines. Allocation diag-nostics and controlled ablations further distinguish the contribution of non-uniform redistribution from the addi-tional benefit of meaningful importance alignment. We do not claim universal superiority over specialized ℓ1

attacks. Adapted ℓ1-APGD remains stronger in the separate reference comparisons reported in Appendix M. Rather, our results identify perturbation allocation as a distinct optimiza-tion dimension that can substantially improve matched adver-sarial optimizers under the capacity-limited setting studied here. 

Related Work 

Gradient-based adversarial optimization. Gradient-based attacks have advanced through improvements in op-timization procedures, attack objectives, and evaluation pro-tocols (Croce et al. 2021; Cinà et al. 2025). Projected gra-dient descent (PGD) became a standard approach for norm-constrained adversarial optimization (Madry et al. 2019), while methods such as C&W (Carlini and Wagner 2017) and DeepFool (Moosavi-Dezfooli, Fawzi, and Frossard 2016) explored alternative objective and boundary-based formu-lations. Auto-PGD (APGD) further improves iterative opti-mization through adaptive step-size control and related sta-bilization mechanisms, and forms a core component of Au-toAttack (Croce and Hein 2020). Probability Margin Attack (PMA) shows that attack strength can also depend strongly on the adversarial objective itself, using a probability-space margin rather than a logit-space formulation (Xie et al. 2025). Our work complements these directions by studying how a shared perturbation budget is allocated across the input under local capacity constraints. 

ℓ1 and sparse attacks. Sparse and ℓ1-oriented attacks ex-plicitly exploit the geometry of perturbation concentration (Croce et al. 2022). EAD incorporates an ℓ1 penalty through an elastic-net formulation (Chen et al. 2018), while Sparse-Fool uses local decision-boundary geometry to construct per-turbations affecting relatively few input components (Modas, Moosavi-Dezfooli, and Frossard 2019b). Croce and Hein studied sparse attacks with additional component-wise mag-nitude constraints (Croce and Hein 2019), and later intro-duced ℓ1-APGD, which adaptively controls update sparsity to better exploit ℓ1 geometry (Croce and Hein 2019). Fast Minimum-Norm (FMN) attacks similarly support ℓ1 opti-mization by adapting the norm constraint while searching for small adversarial perturbations (Pintor et al. 2021). Our setting is closely related because it combines a shared 

ℓ1-type budget with coordinate-wise magnitude limits. We do not treat these local capacity constraints themselves as the novelty of the work. Instead, we study the optimization problem that arises within such a feasible set: how the shared budget should be distributed when individual coordinates cannot absorb arbitrarily large perturbations. 

Importance-guided and structured perturbations. Prior attacks have used saliency or spatial structure to influence where perturbations are applied. JSMA constructs a saliency map to identify influential input features (Papernot et al. 2015b); GreedyFool combines gradient information with a distortion map to select candidate pixels for sparse modifica-tion (Dong et al. 2020a); and superpixel-guided or saliency-based attacks restrict or prioritize perturbations in informa-tive spatial regions (Dong et al. 2020c; Dai et al. 2022). More generally, gradient-based saliency provides a simple estimate of local model sensitivity (Simonyan, Vedaldi, and Zisserman 2014). Structured adversarial attacks additionally impose spatial, group-sparse, or related regularities on the perturbation (Xu et al. 2019). Our mechanism differs from hard support selection or importance-dependent constraints. The importance map does not determine which coordinates are feasible and does not increase the admissible magnitude of high-importance loca-tions. Instead, it enters the attack objective as a soft, centered preference that encourages perturbation mass to be redis-tributed toward more important regions while retaining ac-cess to the same feasible set. This positioning makes the contribution narrower than in-troducing a new saliency attack or a new capacity constraint: we study perturbation allocation as a distinct optimization component under a shared-budget, locally constrained threat model. 

Importance-Guided Perturbation Budget Allocation 

We consider adversarial optimization under a shared global perturbation budget and coordinate-wise magnitude con-straints. The feasible set specifies which perturbations are ad-missible, while our allocation objective provides a soft prefer-ence over where the available budget should be spent. Model-derived importance affects only this optimization preference and does not modify the admissible perturbation region.

Attack Optimization 

Starting from a feasible initialization δ0, we directly optimize Equation 8. At iteration t, we compute 

gt = ∇δ Jours (δt), (10) normalize the gradient by its mean absolute magnitude, and accumulate momentum following momentum-based adver-sarial optimization (Dong et al. 2018). The perturbation is updated in the sign direction of the accumulated gradient, with a cosine-decayed update magnitude (Loshchilov and Hutter 2017). After each update, feasibility is restored using the same constraint operator employed for the matched attacks. We sequentially 1. clip the perturbation to the coordinate-wise capacities E;2. enforce the valid image range x + δ ∈ [0 , 1] D ; and 3. project onto the global ℓ1 ball of radius Db .The final step uses the standard Euclidean projection onto an ℓ1 ball (Duchi et al. 2008). Initialization, step-size, mo-mentum, restart, and projection details are provided in Ap-pendix B. The corresponding exact-projection control is re-ported in Appendix B.4.

Experimental Setup 

Our main evaluation compares attacks within a common capacity-limited feasible set . For each model and evaluation sample, all matched methods use the same global pertur-bation budget, local-capacity realization, image-range con-straints, and evaluation population. A separate comparison between conventional and capacity-limited ℓ1 settings is re-ported descriptively in Appendix M; because those settings use different global budget parameterizations, it is not used to isolate the effect of local capacities.

Matched Baselines 

We compare against three gradient-based baselines adapted to the same capacity-limited feasible set: 

• APGD-CE , using cross-entropy; 

• APGD-DLR , using the difference-of-logits-ratio objec-tive (Croce and Hein 2020); and 

• PMA , using the probability-margin objective (Xie et al. 2025). The adaptations enforce the common constraints while retaining each baseline’s characteristic optimization proce-dure or objective. Our reference attack uses untargeted cross-entropy augmented with the centered allocation reward in Equation 9. Unless otherwise stated, all attacks use 50 optimization steps and one restart. Full adaptation details and attack hy-perparameters are provided in Appendix D and Appendix F. These baselines are used specifically to test allocation guidance under a matched feasible set. Results for special-ized ℓ1-APGD and FMN-ℓ1 are reported separately in Ap-pendix M and are not part of the universal baseline compar-ison. 

Allocation-Weight Selection 

We select the allocation strength λ independently for each dataset using 100 held-out clean-correct samples. The can-didate set is {0.10 , 0.20 , 0.35 , 0.50 , 0.75 }, with ASR used as the selection criterion. This yields λ = 0 .5 for CIFAR-10, 

λ = 0 .5 for CIFAR-100, and λ = 0 .2 for ImageNet. The selected value is then frozen across models and re-ported evaluations within that dataset. Complete selection and sensitivity results are given in Appendix G.

Because methods within each configuration attack the same clean-correct inputs, performance comparisons are paired. Computational Accounting and Reproducibility 

We measure computational cost using wall-clock runtime and forward/backward model evaluations. Constructing the clean-gradient importance prior requires one additional back-ward evaluation before iterative optimization. A compute-matched control tests whether this additional evaluation ex-plains the observed gain. Full timing and evaluation counts are reported in Appendix K. Hyperparameters, projection details, and constraint-validity checks are reported in Appendix F, Ap-pendix B, and Appendix N.

Optimization Controls 

Several controls test alternative explanations for the observed gain. First, the effect is not specific to cross-entropy. Adding the centered allocation mechanism improves the correspond-ing unguided attack for all four evaluated objectives—cross-entropy, margin, DLR, and probability margin—on all three reference datasets. Moreover, the strongest guided objective differs across datasets: margin reaches 23 .1% on CIFAR-10, DLR reaches 42 .0% on CIFAR-100, and probability margin reaches 43 .8% on ImageNet. This indicates that allocation guidance is complementary to the choice of adversarial loss rather than specific to the reference cross-entropy objective. Second, compute matching does not explain the result. Replacing one of the 50 iterative attack steps with the clean-gradient computation yields the same reference ASRs as the 

50 + 1 configuration: 19 .6% , 37 .9% , and 36 .8% on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet. Third, replacing the sequential feasibility operator with the exact projection changes ASR only marginally; full results are reported in Appendix B.4. Finally, on the CIFAR-10 and ImageNet reference con-trols, replacing the heterogeneous capacity envelope with a uniform 12 /255 cap produces the same ASR as the hetero-geneous setting. This suggests that the observed allocation gain in these controls is not specific to the sampled capac-ity heterogeneity. Capacity-structure details are reported in Appendix C, while the remaining mechanism controls are reported in Appendix H. 

Computational Cost and Perturbation Diagnostics 

Our method requires one additional backward evaluation to construct the clean-gradient prior: 51 backward evaluations versus 50 for the matched baselines. It uses 103 forward eval-uations, compared with 102 for PMA and 53 for the APGD variants. Recorded wall-clock runtime is consequently close to PMA, while APGD remains cheaper in forward-evaluation count. Complete timing results are reported in Appendix K. The stronger allocation concentration also changes the lo-cal perturbation profile. Although Ours does not consume a larger global mean-ℓ1 budget, it exhibits larger local magni-tudes and greater perceptual distortion than the more diffuse matched baselines. The same pattern is observed in paired perceptual comparisons restricted to common-success ex-amples. We therefore interpret the performance gain as an allocation effect, not as a simultaneous improvement in per-ceptual quality. Full ℓ1, ℓ∞, high-quantile magnitude, LPIPS, DISTS, and SSIM diagnostics are reported in Appendix J. 

Scope and Limitations 

Our results support a specific claim about perturbation al-location within the evaluated capacity-limited threat model. The main comparisons use matched APGD- and PMA-based baselines under the same global budget, local capacities, image-range constraints, and evaluation samples. Within this setting, importance-guided allocation improves attack suc-cess across all ten model–dataset configurations. We do not claim universal superiority over specialized ℓ1 attacks: adapted ℓ1-APGD remains stronger in the reference compar-isons in Appendix M. The conventional and capacity-limited results in Ap-pendix M are descriptive rather than causal because the set-tings differ in coordinate-wise capacities and global budget parameterization. Our threat model represents one family of locally constrained ℓ1 settings. Although a uniform-capacity control gives similar results, other capacity structures may behave differently. Our allocation prior is a fixed clean-gradient importance map computed once before the attack, so the study does not establish clean gradients as the optimal allocation signal. Dynamic, learned, or alternative estimators remain possible extensions. The main experiments use 50 optimization steps and one restart, and do not exhaust substantially larger com-pute budgets. Finally, λ is selected per dataset on a held-out calibration set and then frozen across models. Stronger importance con-centration does not monotonically improve attack success, so the selected values are practical operating points rather than globally optimal settings. The method can also increase lo-cal perturbation magnitude and perceptual distortion while remaining within the same global mean-ℓ1 budget; corre-sponding diagnostics are reported in Appendix J. Overall, explicit allocation guidance can materially im-prove matched adversarial optimization under the evaluated shared-budget, locally constrained setting, without establish-ing universal superiority over specialized ℓ1 attacks or the causal effect of local capacities across separately parameter-ized threat regimes. Conclusion 

We studied adversarial optimization under a shared global mean-ℓ1 budget with coordinate-wise capacity constraints, focusing on the allocation of perturbation within a fixed feasible set. We introduced a centered importance-guided allocation objective that uses a fixed model-derived prior to steer pertur-bation toward more informative regions without changing the admissible perturbation set. Across ten robust model–dataset configurations, this mechanism improves attack success over matched APGD- and PMA-based baselines under the same capacity-limited constraints. Allocation diagnostics and ab-lations indicate that both centered non-uniform redistribution and alignment with model-derived importance contribute to the gain. These results identify perturbation allocation as a distinct optimization dimension under shared-budget, locally con-strained threat models. They do not imply universal supe-riority over specialized ℓ1 attacks, but suggest that explicit allocation guidance can be a useful component of future ad-versarial optimizers.

K Computational Cost 

Table 20 reports model-evaluation counts and dataset-level macro-averaged attack runtime. L Statistical Analysis 

All main attack comparisons are paired because methods within a dataset–model configuration are evaluated on the same clean-correct examples. For each baseline B, we define the paired ASR effect 

∆ASR = ASR ours − ASR B . (52) We report paired 95% confidence intervals produced by the evaluation pipeline. Differences in paired binary success outcomes are tested using the exact McNemar test (McNemar 1947). The family of main comparisons is corrected using Holm’s procedure (Holm 1979). All 30 paired ASR differences are positive, all reported confidence intervals exclude zero, and all comparisons re-main significant after the global Holm correction. The largest corrected p-value is approximately 1.08 × 10 −18 .Because the evaluation populations are large, we empha-size the effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than the magnitude of extremely small p-values.
