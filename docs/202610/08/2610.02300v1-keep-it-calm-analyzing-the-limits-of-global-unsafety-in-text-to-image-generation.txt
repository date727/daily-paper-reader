Keep It CALM: Analyzing the Limits of Global
Unsafety in Text-to-Image Generation
NaHyeon Park1
Minhyun Lee2
Hyunjung Shim1
1KAIST
2Samsung Electronics
Abstract
Training-free safeguards for text-to-image generation often rely on a reusable
safety signal, such as an unsafe direction or global toxic subspace, applied broadly
across prompts. We provide a controlled geometric analysis of this global-unsafety
assumption and reveal a consistent coverage-selectivity trade-off: compact unsafe
subspaces fail to cover heterogeneous unsafe semantics, whereas broader aggrega-
tion increasingly distorts safety-adjacent benign prompts. Motivated by this finding,
we propose CALM (Counterfactual Adaptive Local Modulation), a training-free
safeguard that replaces uniform global removal with prompt-local counterfactual
correction. Using matched unsafe-benign anchors, CALM routes each prompt
to active unsafe categories, minimally edits only violating token representations
toward the safe side, and suppresses positively aligned unsafe residual components.
Across broad evaluation, CALM significantly improves unsafe content suppression
while preserving benign utility, demonstrating that local counterfactual correction
provides a more selective alternative to global unsafe signal removal. Code is
available at https://github.com/nahyeonkaty/calm.

Introduction
Text-to-image models [21, 39, 44, 50, 53] have become a central interface for high-fidelity visual
generation, enabling creative and practical use with natural-language prompts. The same open-ended
controllability, however, makes these systems difficult to safeguard. Deployed safety filters can be
bypassed [43], and recent attacks show that unsafe visual content can often be elicited even from
models with dedicated guardrails, through jailbreaks [7, 15, 32, 38, 49, 56], red-teaming [8, 24, 52],
and concept recovery [48, 59]. These failures highlight safe generation as a core challenge for
deploying generative visual systems in open-ended, user-facing settings.
Existing defenses intervene at different stages of the generation pipeline. Training-time and model-
editing methods modify model parameters or internal representations through safety-oriented align-
ment [33] or restricted-concept removal and unlearning [3, 6, 12, 22, 30, 31, 54]. Inference-time
safeguards instead keep the generator fixed and intervene through prompt moderation [58], text
encoding [34, 57], or latent editing [18, 19, 23, 29, 37, 46]. This modularity makes inference-time
safeguards attractive for deployed systems. However, many training-free safeguards reuse a fixed
safety signal across prompts, such as a category-level unsafe direction [19, 46], a negative guidance
rule [2, 5, 34], or a global toxic subspace [57]. This raises a central question: is unsafety sufficiently
compact and transferable to be treated as a single global object of intervention?
A useful safeguard must solve a selective intervention problem: it should suppress harmful visual
evidence when present, while preserving benign prompts that may otherwise trigger false-positive
suppression. For reusable inference-time signals, this requires both coverage (i.e., broad transfer
across heterogeneous harmful prompts) and selectivity (i.e., minimal distortion of nearby benign se-
mantics). We examine whether these two requirements hold in the frozen text-encoder representation

space used by text-to-image models. Using controlled diagnostics based on subspace angles [4, 60],
projector overlap [9, 13], spectral effective rank [16, 45], and nearest-neighbor preservation, we find
that pooled unsafe geometry faces a persistent coverage–selectivity trade-off.
Specifically, the coverage side analysis reveals several limitations of global unsafe geometry. Unsafe
category subspaces are sensitive to finite lexical sampling, and different unsafe categories share only
weak compact overlap, suggesting that unsafety is not a stable global object. Moreover, pooled
subspaces estimated from seen unsafe categories transfer unevenly to unseen categories. A natural
response is to increase the rank of the pooled subspace. However, broader projection exposes the
selectivity side of the trade-off: it increasingly perturbs safety-adjacent benign concepts, such as
medical emergency, historical conflict, and combat sports. Thus, the core issue is the use of a single
prompt-independent removal object: heterogeneous unsafe content is collapsed into one target and
removed in a coarse-grained, undifferentiated manner.

These findings motivate a local minimal-intervention view of
inference-time safety. Rather than compressing heterogeneous un-
safe anchors into a single direction or subspace for global deletion,
we keep matched unsafe–benign counterfactual pairs as the basic
unit of safety information. This counterfactual structure specifies not
only what to remove, but also where to move: toward the matched
safe side of the local unsafe evidence. Fig. 1 illustrates this distinc-
tion: global removal suppresses a broad unsafe region, whereas local
counterfactual pairs enable moving prompt-specific unsafe evidence
toward its matched benign side. To this end, we introduce CALM
(Counterfactual Adaptive Local Modulation), a training-free safe-
guard that implements this principle with matched unsafe–benign
anchors. Given a prompt, CALM routes it to a sparse set of active
unsafe categories using unsafe–benign counterfactual margins. It
then edits only token representations that violate the routed safe half-spaces (i.e., the safe sides of
local boundaries), projecting them minimally toward the matched benign side. During denoising, it
uses the same routed counterfactuals to suppress positively aligned unsafe residual components. Our
method does not require any external safety classifier, reward model, or image-level feedback.
We evaluate CALM along three axes: harmful-prompt mitigation, benign utility, and backbone
transfer. Across broad unsafe-prompt benchmarks including adversarial prompt attacks [55, 59],
CALM provides strong unsafe-content suppression on SD-v1.4 while maintaining text fidelity and
general benign utility. A multi-category unsafety evaluation on I2P benchmark [46] and ViSU [40]
further shows that the routed counterfactual design improves safety across heterogeneous harm
categories, rather than only on a single narrow attack type. Beyond SD-v1.4, CALM also reduces
unsafe generations on recent backbones, including SDXL [39], FLUX.1 [20], SANA1.5 [53], and
OmniGen2 [50]. These results suggest that local counterfactual correction is a lightweight and
modular alternative to global unsafe-signal removal.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we provide a controlled geometric analysis showing that pooled
unsafe removal faces a practical coverage–selectivity trade-off: compact global subspaces under-
cover held-out unsafe categories, whereas broader projection increasingly distorts safety-adjacent
benign semantics. Second, we introduce CALM, a training-free prompt-responsive safeguard
that uses matched unsafe-benign counterfactuals to define local safe sides and perform minimal
correction toward safe generation. Third, we evaluate CALM across harmful-prompt, benign-utility,
multi-category, and backbone-transfer settings, with ablations isolating the effectiveness of each
component.

Related Work
Safe text-to-image generation.
Safety interventions for text-to-image diffusion models can be
grouped by where they act in the generation pipeline. Training-time and model-editing methods align
diffusion models toward safer generations [28, 33] or remove restricted concepts from pretrained
models [3, 10, 11, 12, 22, 31, 40]. Inference-time safeguards instead keep the generator largely
fixed and intervene through prompt moderation [58], latent-space detection [29], detector-guided
generation [23], safe denoising [18], or dynamic safety guidance [19, 37]. These methods are
2


attractive because they can be applied modularly without retraining the generator. However, many
inference-time safeguards predefine a reusable safety signal by aggregating a number of unsafe
categories. Our work scrutinizes this assumption and shows when such reuse becomes unreliable.
Reusable safety signals in text-to-image models.
Text-to-image generation can be steered via
semantic guidance [5], latent directions [25], or negative prompting [2]. Safety methods reuse such
signals to suppress harmful content [3, 34, 46, 57]: SLD [46] guides generation with category-level
unsafe concepts, while SAFREE [57] projects text-token representations away from a global toxic
subspace. These methods implicitly assume that unsafe semantics share transferable geometric
structure across prompts or categories. Recent safeguards further build on SLD- or SAFREE-style
signals by adding denoising correction (SafeD) [18], flow guidance (SGF) [19], text embedding
guidance (STG) [34], or category-adaptive routing (CASG [51]; discussed more in App. E.1). Our
work instead examines this shared assumption directly: we trace the coverage–selectivity trade-off
to its geometric source and replace uniform unsafe removal with prompt-dependent counterfactual
correction.
3
Limits of Global Unsafe Subspaces
Many inference-time safeguards [34, 46, 57, 51] estimate a safety-related geometric signal once and
reuse it across prompts. We study this assumption in the frozen text-encoder space, the interface
through which every prompt enters a text-to-image model and where many training-free safeguards
define their signal. Since our analysis lives in the text space, it directly targets SAFREE-style
projection, though SLD-style guidance rests on the same assumption of a reusable unsafe direction.
For such a subspace to be a valid object of intervention, it must satisfy two requirements: (i) coverage
(i.e., capturing unsafe semantics stably and transferring to unsafe content beyond the anchors it was
fit on) and (ii) selectivity (i.e., leaving benign semantics intact). Sec. 3.2 shows that pooled subspaces
fall short on coverage, and Sec. 3.3 shows that broader removal sacrifices selectivity. In other words,
we show that a fixed global subspace faces an inherent trade-off between the two due to the geometry.

3.2
Coverage: pooled unsafe subspaces transfer unevenly
We examine coverage from three angles: whether the subspace estimated for a single category is
robust, whether different categories share structure that pooling can exploit, and whether the pooled
subspace transfers to categories it was not fit on (Fig. 2).
(1) Is a category subspace stable under lexical sampling? For each unsafe category and sample
size n ∈{5, 10, 20}, we draw two disjoint subsets of lexical anchors, estimate a rank-k subspace
from each, and measure their alignment by principal angles. The angles vary strongly with n,
large for small subsets and shrinking as more anchors are added (Fig. 2a), which shows that the
estimated subspace depends on how many and which anchors were drawn. Even at larger n, the angle
distribution of unsafe categories is indistinguishable from that of benign categories estimated the
same way. Unsafe subspaces thus carry no distinctive stability of their own: they behave like any
other category subspace, and their geometry reflects the anchor list as much as the unsafe semantics.
(2) Do unsafe categories share common structure? Pooling presumes that categories overlap in a
low-dimensional component. We estimate a subspace per category and compare pairwise principal
angles against two references: unrelated benign categories, which share no structure, and AKC-style
groups of Stanford Dogs breeds [17], which form a coherent family (see App. A.1). Unsafe categories
are similar to the unrelated benign ones, not like the dog-breed groups (Fig. 2b), suggesting that
pooling aggregates heterogeneous categories rather than isolating a common component.
(3) Does a pooled subspace transfer to unseen categories? Lastly, we test the generalization directly
by holding out each category c, fitting a rank-k pooled subspace U (k)

3.3
Selectivity: broader removal perturbs benign semantics
If a pooled subspace covers too little, the natural fix is to remove more: raise its rank or add unsafe
terms. This is harmless only if unsafe terms concentrate in a few dominant directions. However,
they do not. Individual categories have effective rank around 22, and the seven-category pooled set
has effective rank 187.47. Covering pooled unsafe terms by uniform projection therefore requires
removing many directions, and selectivity hinges on whether benign semantics live in those directions.
We answer this in two steps: which benign concepts sit near the unsafe boundary, and whether pooled
projection actually disturbs them.

held-out unsafe terms with a TPR of 0.945, so it captures a meaningful unsafe boundary. However,
safety-adjacent benign terms cross this boundary far more often than general benign controls (false
positive rate 0.270 vs. 0.034, as shown in Fig. 3a). These indicate that harmful and allowed meanings
share the neighborhoods, so any direction that covers the former also passes through the latter.
(5) Does pooled projection perturb them? We project embeddings onto the complement of a pooled
unsafe subspace and renormalize. To measure how much semantics survive, we compare each term
before and after projection by cosine similarity and by how many of its top-10 nearest neighbors
it keeps, weighting closer neighbors more heavily so that the score reflects local semantic stability.
Unsafe terms are perturbed most, as the projection intends. But because safety-adjacent benign terms
sit close to them, they are perturbed next, losing more similarity and more neighbors than general
benign controls (Fig. 3b). The damage grows with the removal subspace: higher-rank projection
further reduces preservation for safety-adjacent terms and begins to erode general benign terms as
well (Fig. 3c), so broader removal is not confined to the unsafe neighborhood. We find that these
trends also hold for other text encoders (e.g., T5 and larger OpenCLIP), where the results are in
App. A.6.
Takeaway.
These results locate the coverage–selectivity trade-off in the geometry of the text space:
unsafe semantics are too heterogeneous for a compact subspace to cover, and too entangled with
safety-adjacent benign semantics for a broad one to remove selectively. No fixed removal target
satisfies both, so the intervention object itself must be prompt-dependent: the correction should be
determined by the unsafe semantics each prompt carries, which motivates Sec. 4.
4
CALM: Routed Counterfactual Correction
The analysis in Sec. 3 indicates what an effective intervention object should look like. Because unsafe
semantics are heterogeneous, no single compact subspace covers them, so the object should be a
collection of local targets. Because unsafe and safety-adjacent benign semantics share directions,
deleting those directions perturbs both; the object should instead specify where an unsafe meaning
should move, namely toward its benign counterpart. Finally, the object should act only where a
prompt provides unsafe evidence and leave the rest untouched. CALM meets these three requirements
with matched unsafe–benign pairs. Each pair defines a local mode that maps an unsafe meaning to its
benign counterpart, and at inference CALM routes each prompt to the modes it supports and applies
only the corresponding corrections.

Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setup
Backbone models.
We primarily evaluate CALM on Stable Diffusion v1.4 [44], following common
settings in prior works [18, 19, 46, 57]. Unless otherwise stated, all SD-v1.4 experiments use the
same DDPM scheduler, random seeds (42 unless otherwise specified by the dataset), and 50 denoising
steps across methods. We also conduct a preliminary transfer study on newer backbones, including
SDXL [39], FLUX.1 [20], SANA1.5-4.8B [53], and OmniGen2 [50]. For these models, we use each
backbone’s native sampling configuration and directly implement the widely used inference-time
safeguards [46, 57] for comparison.
Implementation details.
We route each prompt to at most K = 3 categories with smooth-maximum
temperature γr = 0.1 and apply text-side correction with λtext = 0.05. Residual-side correction uses
γres = 3 with norm cap η = 1 and is applied during the first 30% of denoising steps.
Baselines.
We compare CALM against a wide variety of safeguards. For model-editing methods,
we include ESD [10], UCE [11], and Safe-CLIP [40]. For inference-time methods, we include
SLD [46], SAFREE [57], and STG [34], as well as SafeD [18] and SGF [19] applied on top of SLD
and SAFREE. The unmodified generator is included as the unprotected reference for each benchmark.
Datasets and metrics.
Safeguards are tested on Ring-A-Bell [48], UnlearnDiff-Atk [59], and
MMA-Diffusion [55]. Following prior work, we report the attack success rate (ASR) and toxicity rate
(TR), as measured by the NudeNet classifier [35], with lower values indicating better performance.
As Ring-A-Bell contains normal prompts that correspond to harmful prompts, we further measure
text fidelity score [41]. For general benign utility, we report FID [14] and CLIP-T [41] on 10k images
from COCO [26], following previous studies [18, 19]. For the multi-category evaluation, we test on
I2P [46] and ViSU [40] and use Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [1] as a vision-language model (VLM) judge
to assess category-wise harmful content; details and additional results are in App. C.

6
Discussion & Conclusion
Many training-free safeguards for text-to-image diffusion assume that unsafe semantics share a
common subspace that can be identified once and removed from every prompt. We showed that this
assumption breaks down because unsafe categories are inherently heterogeneous. Collapsing them
into a single global subspace induces a coverage–selectivity trade-off: a compact subspace misses
diverse unsafe semantics, while a broader one disrupts safety-adjacent benign prompts.
CALM instead performs prompt-routed counterfactual correction. It represents unsafe–benign
structure as a mixture of local modes, routes each prompt only to the modes it activates, and applies a
minimal edit toward its benign counterfactual. Without any training, CALM improves the safety–
preservation trade-off over prior model-editing and inference-time baselines and transfers well to
recent backbones. More broadly, our findings suggest that training-free safeguards should consider
not only how strongly to intervene but also where, since a reusable safety signal need not be global.
Limitations and future work.
Like other inference-time safeguards, CALM is best viewed as one
layer of protection rather than a complete defense. Adversarially optimized prompts such as MMA-
Diffusion and adaptive white-box attacks with full access to the anchor bank or routing thresholds
remain more challenging, and improving robustness in this setting is a promising direction. Finally,
our analysis is conducted in the text encoder space, and extending it to the internal representations of
the denoiser can be a natural next step. We hope this work encourages future safeguards to identify
the right object of intervention before deciding how to intervene.

A.1
Text encoder, lexical sets, and controls
Unless stated otherwise, all representation-space diagnostics use the Stable Diffusion v1.4 text
encoder. Its pooled text representation has dimension d = 768. ϕ(p) = [h1, . . . , hT ] ∈RT ×d
denotes token-level text representations and ¯ϕ(p) ∈Rd denotes the pooled representation of prompt
p. In the lexical diagnostics below, each lexical string s is represented by its pooled embedding
¯ϕ(s) ∈Rd. Embeddings are ℓ2-normalized before cosine, subspace-overlap, and nearest-neighbor
computations. Randomized diagnostics use seed 42.
We use the seven coarse unsafe categories from I2P [46]: harassment, hate, illegal activity, self-
harm, sexual content, shocking content, and violence. For each category, we expand the category by
constructing 30 unsafe terms or short phrases by an LLM [36]. We also construct benign control sets
of diverse categories, also suggested by the same LLM, with comparable size and lexical granularity.
It is important to note that the lexical sets are fixed before evaluation and are not tuned on benchmark
test prompts. We note that we also verified that the main diagnostic trends remain consistent under
alternative lexical variants and random resampling of anchors, suggesting that the findings are not
driven by a particular hand-selected vocabulary.
For the coverage diagnostics in Fig. 2, we use two benign references. The heterogeneous benign
reference consists of semantically diverse benign categories matched in size and phrase granularity
to the unsafe categories. The homogeneous benign reference is constructed by regrouping Stanford
Dogs breed labels [17] into AKC-style breed groups and applying the same subspace-estimation
procedure. The dog-breed reference is used only as a controlled benign comparison for Fig. 2; it is
not used to tune CALM, construct the counterfactual bank, or evaluate safety.
We use centered subspaces for diagnostics about category-internal variation and cross-category align-
ment, because those tests ask whether residual semantic variation within or across categories is stable.
We use uncentered subspaces for projection diagnostics, because projection-based interventions act
directly on the embedding vectors used by the model.

semantic structure. We therefore interpret angles and overlaps relative to matched benign controls
and random-subspace references. If U, V ∈Rd×k are independent random rank-k subspaces, then
E ov(U, V ) = k/d. Although the main text reports principal angles, the normalized overlap shows
nearly identical trends (Tab. 5).

A.5
Selectivity diagnostics
The selectivity requirement asks whether broader projection can improve unsafe coverage without
disproportionately disrupting allowed concepts near unsafe regions. We evaluate this using three
diagnostics.
First, we measure spectral compactness of unsafe concept sets. For each unsafe category, we center the
pooled embeddings of its 30 terms and compute the effective rank of the resulting matrix (App. A.2).
We then pool all seven categories into a single set of 210 terms and compute its effective rank in the
same way. This tests whether unsafe concepts are concentrated in a small number of directions or
require broad projection to cover.
Second, we evaluate benign entanglement with unsafe boundaries. We train a linear logistic-regression
classifier to distinguish unsafe terms from unrelated benign terms using their pooled embeddings.
We then evaluate the classifier on held-out unsafe terms, related benign terms, and unrelated benign
terms. Related benign terms include allowed concepts such as combat sports, historical conflict,
action entertainment, medical emergency, hunting, and butchery. This diagnostic tests whether
safety-adjacent benign concepts lie near or across a learned unsafe boundary.

A.6
Results on Different Embedding Spaces
The main analysis in Sec. 3 is conducted in the CLIP ViT-L/14 text-encoder space used by SD-v1.4.
To examine whether the observed trends are specific to this embedding space, we repeat the same
diagnostics with two additional text encoders: a T5 text encoder [42] and the OpenCLIP ViT-bigG/14
text encoder used in SDXL. As shown in Figs. 8 and 9, both encoders exhibit qualitatively consistent
behavior with the main CLIP-based analysis: pooled unsafe projection perturbs unsafe embeddings
most strongly, while safety-adjacent and general benign embeddings are relatively better preserved.
The absolute preservation values differ across encoders, indicating that each text encoder has its own
representation geometry. Nevertheless, the relative ordering and overall trends remain consistent,
suggesting that the observed coverage–selectivity structure is not unique to the CLIP ViT-L/14 space
used in the main analysis.

Additional Method Details
This appendix provides implementation details of Sec. 4. The main method consists of three
operations: prompt routing, text-side safe-half-space projection, and residual-side counterfactual
correction. All hyperparameters are fixed across benchmark prompts.
B.1

D.2
Counterfactual-bank Robustness
Bank construction.
Both the analysis in Sec. 3 and the method in Sec. 4 operate over the seven
standard I2P categories. For each category, we prompt an LLM once, requesting exactly 30 unsafe
items together with 30 matched benign counterparts. No manual selection or curation is performed at
any stage; the bank is fixed prior to evaluation and is never tuned on benchmark prompts. Below we
evaluate whether CALM is sensitive to the size, composition, and generating LLM of the bank.
Bank size and composition.
Following the ablation protocol in Sec. 5, we use 30 prompts randomly
sampled from the harmful-prompt benchmarks. Note that this sample differs from that of Tab. 3,
so absolute values are not directly comparable across the two tables. Tab. 6(a) reports results when
using only 75%, 50%, or 25% of the anchor pairs, averaged over three generation seeds. Even with
substantially reduced banks, ASR remains comparable to the full-bank setting and CLIP-T changes
only marginally, indicating that prompt fidelity is largely preserved. TR shows moderate variation,
but does not degrade monotonically as the bank size decreases. This suggests that CALM benefits
from the counterfactual structure of the bank rather than relying on exhaustive anchor coverage.
Tab. 6(b) further evaluates different randomly sampled 50% bank subsets, where 15 out of 30 anchor
pairs are independently sampled for each category. For this subset-composition test, we use the same
30 prompts and report results over three different bank-subset seeds. The results remain consistent
across subsets, suggesting that CALM does not rely on a carefully selected full bank.
Generating bank with LLM.
To assess sensitivity to the LLM used for bank generation, we
regenerate the entire bank with two additional LLMs (Gemini and Qwen3-8B) using the same query
as the original ChatGPT bank, and re-run all harmful-prompt benchmarks (Tab. 7). Performance is
stable across independently generated banks, and no single bank dominates across all benchmarks.
This suggests that CALM depends on the counterfactual structure (matched unsafe–benign pairs)
rather than on specific word choices. Overall, routed counterfactual correction remains effective
under reduced, resampled, and independently regenerated anchor sets.

D.3
Results on an additional dataset
We additionally evaluate CALM on the ViSU dataset [40], which covers 20 harm categories. Since
ASR and TR rely on a nudity classifier, we report them on the nudity/sexual subset of the ViSU test
split (Tab. 8).
To cover all harm types, we further apply the VLM-judge protocol (Qwen3-VL, identical prompt
format) over all 20 ViSU tags, generating 10 images per tag (200 images per method), and report the
average unsafe rate in Tab. 9.
Safe-CLIP achieves the lowest unsafe rate, which is expected since it is fine-tuned on the ViSU
train split. CALM is second-best without any training or exposure to this dataset, indicating strong
generalization. Notably, SLD, SAFREE, and CALM all draw on the same seven I2P categories,
yet CALM attains a markedly lower unsafe rate, supporting our claim that the gain comes from the
intervention object (routed counterfactual correction) rather than from a richer safety vocabulary.

Additional Discussion
E.1
Relation to CASG
A very recent work, CASG [51] also questions naive aggregation of category-specific safety signals,
which is aligned with our finding that pooled unsafe directions can be unreliable. However, CASG
primarily motivates this issue empirically and selects a single dominant unsafe category for guidance.
In contrast, our work provides a geometric explanation for why pooled unsafe removal faces a
coverage–selectivity trade-off, and CALM addresses prompts containing multiple active harm
categories through routed counterfactual correction.
Tab. 10 further highlights this distinction. While CASG-based variants achieve competitive CLIP-T
scores, they still exhibit high ASR and TR on Ring-A-Bell. In contrast, CALM substantially reduces
harmful generation while maintaining comparable text fidelity. These results suggest that selectively

Instead of selecting one dominant category, CALM routes each prompt to a sparse set of active
categories, constructs matched unsafe–benign counterfactual boundaries, applies token-level safe-side
projection, and reuses the same routed geometry for residual correction. Thus, CALM is not only a
category selector; it changes the intervention target from global unsafe-signal removal to prompt-local
counterfactual correction.
E.2
Broader Impacts
This work studies inference-time safeguards for text-to-image generation, aiming to reduce harmful
visual outputs while preserving benign uses, including educational, medical, artistic, and historical
prompts. A broader implication of our analysis is that safety mechanisms should be evaluated not
only by how strongly they suppress unsafe content, but also by how selectively they preserve allowed
semantics. We hope this perspective supports the development of safer and more reliable generative
visual systems.
This work also carries potential risks. Analyzing the limitations of existing safeguards may expose
weaknesses that could inform adaptive attacks or jailbreak attempts. In addition, definitions of
unsafe and benign content are necessarily incomplete and may vary across cultural, social, and
policy contexts. The fixed counterfactual anchors and VLM-based evaluation used in this paper may
therefore reflect biases or blind spots. Accordingly, CALM should not be treated as a standalone
safety solution, but as one component in a broader safety pipeline that includes complementary
safeguards, policy constraints, and human oversight.
