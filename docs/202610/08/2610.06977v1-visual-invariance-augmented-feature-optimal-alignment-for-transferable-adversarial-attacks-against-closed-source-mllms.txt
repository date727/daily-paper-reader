Abstract—Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) re-
main vulnerable to transferable adversarial examples, especially
in black-box scenarios where only open-source surrogate models
are accessible. Existing targeted transfer attacks mainly align
adversarial and target samples using global image-level features,
such as the [CLS] embedding of vision-language encoders.
However, such coarse alignment insufficiently exploits patch-
level visual structures, leading to limited transferability across
heterogeneous closed-source MLLMs. To address this issue, we
propose a visual-invariance-augmented feature optimal align-
ment attack with adaptive unbalanced transport, called IAU-
FOA, for improving targeted transferability against closed-source
MLLMs. IAU-FOA aligns adversarial and target samples from
both global and local perspectives: a cosine-based objective
narrows their global semantic gap, while patch tokens are clus-
tered into compact local patterns and matched through optimal
transport for fine-grained feature alignment. Balanced optimal
transport enforces fixed marginal masses even for local clusters
without reliable counterparts, potentially introducing misleading
alignment gradients. We therefore introduce confidence-adaptive
unbalanced transport to relax these constraints for weakly
matched clusters, aiming to reduce unreliable local alignment
and improve adversarial transferability. Moreover, we study the
effect of input transformations on adversarial transferability
and propose a visual-invariance augmentation strategy that
applies bidirectional pixel-intensity rescaling and per-channel
white-balance adjustment to simulate exposure, contrast, illumi-
nation, and color-temperature variations, thereby encouraging
adversarial perturbations to generalize across different visual
encoders. Extensive experiments on open-source and closed-
source MLLMs show that IAU-FOA consistently outperforms
state-of-the-art transferable attack methods. The code is released
at https://github.com/jiaxiaojunQAQ/IAU-FOA.
Index Terms—Multimodal large language models, transferable
adversarial attacks, feature optimal alignment, adaptive unbal-
anced optimal transport, visual-invariance augmentation.

Ultimodal large language models (MLLMs) [6, 55]
have become a representative framework for connect-
ing visual perception with language reasoning [15, 21]. In most
current systems, an input image is first encoded into visual
tokens or embeddings by a vision encoder, and these visual
representations are then projected into the language model for
multimodal understanding and generation [22, 29, 33, 46, 62].
In particular, many MLLMs adopt pre-trained vision encoders,
such as Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) [40],
to extract image features and align them with language embed-
dings. This modular design has enabled strong performance
on a wide range of vision-language applications. Beyond
advances in open-source MLLMs, commercial closed-source
MLLMs such as GPT-5.5, Claude-Opus-4.8, and Gemini-3.5-
Flash have also been widely deployed in practical multimodal
services. However, since MLLMs heavily rely on vision en-
coders to perceive visual inputs, they may also inherit the ad-
versarial vulnerabilities of these visual front ends [34, 45, 56].
Recent studies [4, 7, 30, 37] have shown that MLLMs can
be vulnerable to adversarial examples [8], which are typi-
cally generated by adding small and visually imperceptible
perturbations to clean images, such that the perturbed images
remain natural to humans but change the model’s responses.
Such vulnerability raises security and safety concerns for real-
world foundation-model services, where manipulated visual
inputs may mislead downstream multimodal reasoning. Ac-
cording to the attack objective, adversarial attacks on MLLMs
can be broadly divided into untargeted and targeted attacks.
Untargeted attacks aim to induce incorrect responses, whereas
targeted attacks attempt to drive the model toward attacker-
specified semantics or outputs [19, 57].
As illustrated in Fig. 1, targeted adversarial examples can
preserve the visual appearance of the source image while steer-
ing commercial MLLMs toward target-related descriptions. In
closed-source scenarios, the gradients and parameters of target
MLLMs are inaccessible, making direct optimization infeasi-
ble. A practical alternative is to craft adversarial examples on
open-source surrogate models and then transfer them to the
target systems. This property, known as adversarial transfer-
ability [9], measures whether adversarial examples generated
for one model can remain effective on other unseen models.
Transferability is important for both untargeted and targeted
attacks, but it is particularly difficult to achieve in the targeted
black-box setting, where the perturbation must consistently
convey the target semantics across heterogeneous MLLMs

Previous targeted transfer attack methods [23, 24, 45] typ-
ically generate adversarial examples by minimizing a con-
trastive loss between the global features of the adversarial
image and the target image, where the global representation
is often given by the [CLS] token of an open-source image
encoder such as CLIP. By pulling the adversarial image toward
the target image in the surrogate feature space, these methods
can improve targeted transferability to some extent. However,
their adversarial transferability and attack performance against
closed-source MLLMs remain limited, mainly due to two
limitations. First, most existing methods rely primarily on
global image-level features. Although global feature alignment
can guide adversarial examples toward the target semantics, it
compresses the visual content into a single representation and
overlooks rich patch-level structures. As a result, the generated
perturbations may capture coarse semantic similarity while
failing to sufficiently exploit fine-grained visual patterns that
are important for transfer across heterogeneous MLLMs. Even
when local features are aligned, exact marginal constraints can
force matches between clusters without reliable counterparts.
These matches may introduce gradients that favor incidental
similarities in a surrogate encoder rather than correspondences
shared across models, motivating confidence-adaptive local
transport. Second, the choice of input transformations for tar-
geted transfer attacks against MLLMs warrants further inves-
tigation. Existing approaches include spatial transformations
such as resizing and padding in DI [49], as well as pixel-
intensity scaling in SIM [26]. Building on intensity-based
augmentation, we investigate the combination of bidirectional
intensity scaling and channel-wise gain adjustment to diversify
exposure and illumination-color conditions during targeted
feature alignment.
To address these issues, we propose IAU-FOA, a visual-
invariance-augmented feature optimal alignment attack with an
adaptive unbalanced transport method. Given a source image
and a target image, IAU-FOA generates an adversarial per-
turbation by aligning their representations in surrogate vision-
language encoders from both global and local perspectives.
At the global level, IAU-FOA adopts a coarse-grained feature
alignment objective based on cosine similarity, which pulls the
image-level representation of the adversarial example toward
that of the target sample. Since the global representation is
usually encoded by the [CLS] token, this objective provides
a stable semantic direction for targeted attacks and encourages
the adversarial image to approach the target semantics in the
surrogate feature space.
At the local level, IAU-FOA further exploits the patch-
token representations produced by Transformer-based vision
encoders. While the [CLS] token summarizes global infor-
mation, the remaining visual tokens contain local patch-level
details. Directly using all local tokens, however, can introduce
redundancy and noisy correspondences. Therefore, IAU-FOA
first clusters patch tokens into compact and discriminative
local visual patterns, where each cluster center summarizes the
characteristics of a group of related patches. It then formulates
the alignment between adversarial and target local patterns
as an optimal transport problem, enabling fine-grained feature
matching beyond a single global embedding.
To make local alignment more reliable, IAU-FOA fur-
ther introduces an adaptive unbalanced transport mechanism.
Balanced OT requires each cluster to transport its pre-
scribed mass, even when reliable counterparts are absent.
The proposed scheme instead adapts marginal regularization
to cluster-level matching confidence. High-confidence clusters
receive stronger penalties on marginal deviations, while low-
confidence clusters receive weaker penalties. This flexibility
aims to reduce the interference of unreliable local matches
in adversarial optimization and thereby improve adversarial
transferability. In addition, when multiple visual encoders are
used as surrogate models, IAU-FOA computes an alignment
similarity score for each encoder and dynamically adjusts its
weight using the ratio between the current and previously
recorded scores. For positive scores, larger relative increases
receive larger weights, allowing the ensemble to respond to
changes in alignment across surrogate encoders.
Finally, IAU-FOA incorporates a visual-invariance augmen-
tation (VIA) strategy during adversarial optimization. Build-
ing on existing intensity-scaling augmentation, our strategy
combines bidirectional pixel-intensity rescaling with channel-
wise gain adjustment to approximate exposure and white-
balance variations during targeted alignment. By optimizing
adversarial perturbations under these transformed visual con-
ditions, IAU-FOA encourages the perturbations to rely less
on surrogate-specific visual statistics and to remain effective
across heterogeneous closed-source MLLMs. Our main con-
tributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose IAU-FOA, a targeted black-box attack
framework for MLLMs that drives adversarial images
toward target semantics through a dual-granularity feature
matching objective. Specifically, a cosine-based loss pro-
vides coarse semantic guidance from global embeddings,
while clustered patch tokens are matched through an

II. RELATED WORK
A. Multimodal Large Language Models
Large language models (LLMs) [60] have achieved im-
pressive success across a wide range of Natural Language
Processing (NLP) tasks, demonstrating strong capabilities in
language understanding [20], instruction following [13], and
complex reasoning [11, 35]. Building upon these advances,
multimodal large language models (MLLMs) extend LLMs
to vision-language scenarios by integrating visual perception
modules with LLM backbones. Specifically, MLLMs align
visual representations with the semantic space of LLMs,
enabling the language model to interpret visual inputs and
generate grounded textual responses [1, 28, 42]. Representa-
tive systems usually connect pre-trained vision encoders and
LLMs through lightweight alignment modules, such as the Q-
Former in BLIP-2 [22] and the MLP projector in LLaVA [29].
Some open-source models further enhance fine-grained visual
perception through versatile visual instruction tuning and high-
resolution image processing, as demonstrated by Qwen2-
VL [46] and InternVL [3]. Meanwhile, commercial MLLMs,
such as GPT-5.5 and Claude-Opus-4.8, have exhibited pow-
erful multimodal understanding, reasoning, and instruction-
following capabilities, representing the leading performance
of current vision-language systems. However, their closed-
source nature, together with substantial differences in model
architectures, vision encoders, alignment modules, and training
recipes, makes their internal representation spaces difficult to
access or approximate, posing challenges for black-box attacks
against modern MLLMs.
B. Adversarial Attacks
Adversarial attacks aim to mislead deep neural networks by
adding carefully crafted perturbations to clean inputs [8, 43].
According to the attacker’s access to the victim model, existing
adversarial attack methods are commonly categorized into
white-box attacks, where model parameters or gradients are
available, and black-box attacks, where the attacker has no
access to internal model parameters, gradients, or feature
representations [36, 58]. Depending on the attack objective,
adversarial attacks can also be divided into untargeted at-
tacks and targeted attacks. Untargeted attacks only require
the model to produce incorrect outputs, whereas targeted
attacks aim to induce specific target semantics or responses.
Compared with conventional adversarial attacks on image
classification models, attacking MLLMs is substantially more
challenging [16, 19]. Classification models usually produce
predictions over a fixed label space, allowing attacks to op-
timize well-defined objectives. In contrast, MLLMs generate
open-ended textual responses conditioned on both visual and
textual inputs, where the attack goal is often expressed at
the semantic or instruction-following level rather than as
a discrete label. For closed-source MLLMs, the challenge
becomes more pronounced because their parameters, gradi-
ents, and intermediate feature representations are unavailable
to attackers. A potential solution is to exploit adversarial
transferability [31, 39], where adversarial examples generated
on accessible surrogate models remain effective on unseen
target models. This property enables transfer-based attacks to
probe closed-source MLLMs without directly accessing their
internal states, making it a practical and promising route for
targeted black-box attacks [26, 49].

typically optimize adversarial perturbations on accessible sur-
rogate models, such as CLIP, and transfer them to closed-
source MLLMs. Specifically, Guo et al. [12] propose AdvD-
iffVLM, which leverages diffusion models to generate natural

III. METHOD
Adversarial perturbations learned from a single surrogate
may capture non-transferable representation biases, which can
be alleviated by optimizing over multiple surrogate image
encoders to emphasize shared feature directions across mod-
els [41, 45, 52]. Following this perspective, IAU-FOA adopts
a dynamic ensemble framework for targeted transfer attacks
against closed-source MLLMs. The proposed framework in-
tegrates dual-granularity feature optimal alignment, adaptive
unbalanced local transport, dynamic encoder weighting, and
visual-invariance augmentation (VIA). By jointly aligning
target semantics across multiple surrogate visual encoders
and adapting their weights to relative changes in alignment
similarity, IAU-FOA encourages adversarial perturbations to
capture transferable visual cues shared by foundation models.

C. Visual-Invariance Augmentation
Real imaging conditions and closed-source MLLM visual
preprocessing pipelines often introduce photometric variations,
such as changes in exposure, brightness, illumination color,
and white balance. Accurately modeling the full physical im-
age formation process is difficult for transferable optimization.
Instead, we use two simple, differentiable, and low-cost oper-
ations, namely global intensity scaling and channel-wise gain
adjustment, to cover these common visual variations. T (xadv)
denotes a transformed view of the adversarial image. To keep
the notation compact, we use T as a generic augmentation
operator in the overall objective, and instantiate it here with
photometric transformations. Specifically, each transformation
combines an intensity scale s and a channel-wise gain vector
β = [βR, βG, βB]⊤. We enumerate the intensity scale s over
S = {2k | k = −h, . . . , h},
(6)
where h is a nonnegative integer controlling the range of
intensity scaling, and k indexes the 2h + 1 scales. Scaling
darkens (s < 1), brightens (s > 1), or preserves (s = 1) image
intensity. By default, h = 2, giving five bidirectional intensity
scales S = {0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4}. Given s ∈S and β ∈R3, the
concrete operator used in Eq. (5) is

The global scale s approximates changes in brightness
and exposure, while the channel gains β approximate white-
balance and illumination-color shifts. The transformation set
B in Eq. (5) is constructed by enumerating s ∈S and
sampling a channel-gain vector β for each scale. These
transformations simulate exposure, illumination, and color-
temperature changes. In each iteration, gradients from all
transformed copies are averaged before updating the pertur-
bation, which encourages the adversarial example to remain
effective under heterogeneous visual preprocessing pipelines.
Each transformed copy is then passed to the surrogate encoders
to generate adversarial examples.

F. Adaptive Unbalanced Optimal Transport
The balanced formulation in Eq. (15) provides a basic
local alignment objective in which every cluster mass is
exactly transported according to fixed marginals. With entropic
regularization, the balanced OT problem can be solved using
Sinkhorn iterations. However, exact marginal preservation
can be too restrictive for targeted adversarial optimization:
background clusters, ambiguous regions, or surrogate-specific
patch patterns may be forced to match target clusters even
when no reliable correspondence exists. Such forced matches
may steer perturbations toward incidental similarities specific
to the surrogate, limiting their transfer to other encoders. We
therefore use an adaptive unbalanced Sinkhorn solver with
weaker marginal regularization for low-confidence clusters
and stronger regularization for high-confidence clusters. This
design aims to reduce unreliable alignment by allowing trans-
ported masses to adapt to the available correspondences.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Setup
Datasets. Following prior studies [4, 17, 24], we adopt 1,000
clean source images resized to 224 × 224 × 3 from the NIPS
2017 Adversarial Attacks and Defenses Competition dataset1.
For the targeted setting, 1,000 images are randomly sampled
from the MSCOCO validation set [27] as target images.
Implementation Settings. Following [23, 45], we gener-
ate adversarial examples using five surrogate models: three
CLIP variants, ViT-B/16 [40], ViT-B/32 [40], and ViT-G/14
(LAION-2B) [40], together with InternVL3-1B [64] and DI-
NOv2 [38]. For IAU-FOA, unless otherwise specified, the
perturbation budget is ϵ = 16/255 under the ℓ∞norm, with
a step size of 1/255 and 300 attack iterations. The number of
local clusters is fixed at K = 10 throughout optimization.
Local cluster reference masses are uniform, and the local-
loss weight is λl = 2.0. Adaptive UOT uses ρ = 0.2,
εot = 0.1, and γ = 1, with mean-normalized confidence
and total-mass normalization of the transport plan. Dynamic
weighting uses τw = 1.0 and a numerical stabilizer of 10−8.
VIA uses intensity scales {0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4} (h = 2) and a
channel-gain amplitude of sβ = 0.5. Our main comparison
covers twelve MLLMs, comprising six open-source models
(Qwen2.5-VL-7B/72B, InternVL3-14B/78B, MiniCPM-o-2.6,
LLaVA-1.6-7B) and six closed-source models (Claude-Sonnet-
4.6/Opus-4.8, GPT-5.4/5.5, Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite/3.5-Flash).
An additional evaluation on five recent commercial MLLMs
brings the total to seventeen targets. For all evaluations, the
prompt is fixed as “Describe this image, no longer than 25
words”. Experiments are conducted on an Ubuntu system
equipped with an NVIDIA H100 Tensor Core GPU with
80 GB of GPU memory.
Competitive Methods. We compare the proposed IAU-FOA
with seven representative targeted transfer attacks for MLLMs,
including AdvDiffVLM [12], AnyAttack [59], COA [50], M-
Attack [24], FOA-Attack [17], M-Attack-v2 [61], and MP-
CAttack [23]. For baselines other than AdvDiffVLM, we use
the same surrogate models, perturbation budget, and attack
iterations as IAU-FOA; the remaining method-specific settings
follow their original papers. AdvDiffVLM follows the settings
of its original paper [12] and is not constrained by the
ℓ∞budget of 16/255. All methods use the same evaluation
protocol described below.
Evaluation metrics. Following [17, 24], we evaluate targeted
transferability with an LLM-as-a-judge protocol. For each
adversarial–target pair, the target MLLM first produces cap-
tions for both images, and GPTScore is then used to quantify
their semantic similarity by using GPT-4o. We regard an
attack as successful when the similarity score is at least 0.5,
indicating that the adversarial image is judged to share the
target subject. In addition to the attack success rate (ASR), we
report the average similarity score (AvgSim) to reflect overall
target alignment; higher values of both metrics indicate better
targeted transferability.

B. Hyper-parameter Selection
All results in Figs. 3 and 4 are evaluated on 100 source–
target image pairs per target model. We investigate the sen-
sitivity of IAU-FOA to three hyperparameters: the marginal-
relaxation penalty weight ρ in adaptive unbalanced OT, the
local-loss weighting factor λl in the dual-granularity alignment
objective, and the number of local clusters K. We vary one
hyperparameter at a time while keeping all other settings fixed.
For ρ and λl, we report the arithmetic means of ASR and
AvgSim over two models in each family: Gemini-3.5-Flash
and Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite for Gemini, GPT-5.5 and GPT-
5.4 for GPT, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6 and Claude-Opus-4.8 for
Claude. For K, we report ASR on all six commercial targets
in the main evaluation.
As shown in Fig. 3(a), we vary ρ over {0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4}.
The setting ρ = 0.2 yields the highest reported ASR and
AvgSim for Gemini, GPT, and Claude, achieving family-
mean ASRs of 85.0%, 81.0%, and 90.5% for Gemini, GPT,
and Claude, respectively, with corresponding mean AvgSim
values of 65.8%, 63.2%, and 68.7%. These family-mean ASRs
agree with the corresponding two-model averages of the full-
method results in Table V. A smaller value may over-relax the
marginal constraints, whereas a larger value moves the trans-
port plan closer to balanced OT and may retain unreliable local
correspondences. Fig. 3(b) evaluates λl ∈{1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0}.
Setting λl = 2.0 also yields the highest reported values of
both metrics for Gemini, GPT, and Claude, with the same
ASR and AvgSim values reported above. These results favor
an intermediate local-loss weight: too little emphasis on local
alignment may limit its contribution, whereas too much may
reduce the benefit of global semantic alignment. Based on
these results, we use ρ = 0.2 and λl = 2.0 in all subsequent
experiments.
Figure 4 further evaluates K ∈{6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16}, which
controls the granularity of the clustered patch features used
for local transport alignment. The highest mean ASR is 0.855
at K = 10, compared with 0.822 and 0.793 at K = 6 and
K = 8, respectively. Increasing K beyond 10 yields mean
ASRs between 0.828 and 0.838, indicating that finer clustering
does not consistently improve aggregate transferability. The
preferred setting nevertheless varies by target: K
= 10
achieves the highest ASR on GPT-5.5, GPT-5.4, and Cl-S-
4.6 and ties for the best result on G-3.5-F, whereas G-3.1-FL
and Cl-O-4.8 peak at K = 14 with ASRs of 0.910 and 0.940,
respectively. Based on the best mean performance across the
six targets, we use a fixed K = 10 throughout optimization
unless otherwise specified.

V. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we investigate targeted adversarial transfer-
ability across heterogeneous MLLMs and propose IAU-FOA
to improve black-box attack performance through global and
local feature alignment. Specifically, global alignment provides
semantic guidance, while optimal transport matches clus-
tered patch features to exploit fine-grained visual structures.
To address unreliable local correspondences under balanced
transport constraints, we propose adaptive unbalanced optimal
transport, which adjusts marginal regularization according to
matching confidence. We also provide a conditional theoretical
analysis of how local transport alignment can improve targeted
transfer. Furthermore, we develop a dynamic ensemble weight-
ing strategy that adjusts each surrogate encoder’s contribution
according to relative changes in its alignment similarity score.
Finally, we propose a visual-invariance augmentation strategy
that combines bidirectional intensity scaling and channel-
wise white-balance adjustment to improve transferability under
varying photometric conditions. Extensive experiments on
open-source and closed-source MLLMs demonstrate that IAU-
FOA outperforms the compared transferable attack methods.
Ablation studies and component analyses further demonstrate
the effectiveness of the proposed designs.

S3. EFFECT OF THE NUMBER OF ATTACK ITERATIONS
All results in Fig. S2 are evaluated on 100 source–target image pairs per target model. Figure S2 evaluates the effect of the
optimization budget by varying the number of attack iterations over {100, 200, 300, 400, 500} while keeping all other settings
fixed. Increasing the budget from 100 to 300 iterations improves ASR on all six commercial MLLMs and raises the mean ASR
from 69.5% to 85.5%, a gain of 16.0 percentage points. Among the tested budgets, 300 iterations achieve the highest mean
ASR. Increasing the budget to 400 iterations lowers ASR on every target and reduces the mean to 82.5%. At 500 iterations,
the mean ASR is 84.5%, still 1.0 percentage point below the result at 300 iterations. Relative to 300 iterations, 500 iterations
improve ASR on G-3.5-F and GPT-5.4, leave it unchanged on Cl-S-4.6, and reduce it on the other three targets. Thus, additional
iterations beyond 300 do not consistently improve targeted transferability. Considering the highest observed mean ASR and the
lower computational cost relative to larger budgets, we retain 300 iterations as the default setting unless otherwise specified.

For reproducibility, Fig. S3 presents the complete prompt used by GPT-4o to compute GPTScore. For each adversarial–target
pair, the two placeholders are replaced with the descriptions generated by the victim MLLM for the adversarial image and the
corresponding target image. The judge is instructed to focus on subject-level semantic consistency, tolerate minor differences
in wording and detail, and return only a scalar score in [0, 1]. We use this scalar directly when computing AvgSim and classify
an attack as successful when the score is at least 0.5. The same prompt and decision rule are applied to all attack methods
and victim models.
Rate the semantic similarity between the following two texts on a scale from 0 to 1. 
Criteria for similarity measurement:                   
1. Main Subject Consistency: If both descriptions refer to the same key subject or object (e.g., a 
person, food, an event), they should receive a higher similarity score.                    
2. Relevant Description: If the descriptions are related to the same context or topic, they should 
also contribute to a higher similarity score.                    
3. Ignore Fine-Grained Details: Do not penalize differences in phrasing, sentence structure, or 
minor variations in detail. Focus on whether both descriptions fundamentally describe the 
same thing.                   
4. Partial Matches: If one description contains extra information but does not contradict the other, 
they should still have a high similarity score.                   
5. Similarity Score Range:                         
- 1.0: Nearly identical in meaning.                       
- 0.8-0.9: Same subject, with highly related descriptions.                        
- 0.7-0.8: Same subject, core meaning aligned, even if some details differ.                        
- 0.5-0.7: Same subject but different perspectives or missing details.                        
- 0.3-0.5: Related but not highly similar (same general theme but different descriptions).      
- 0.0-0.2: Completely different subjects or unrelated meanings.                                            
Text 1: {input_text1}                    
Text 2: {input_text2}                
Output only a single number between 0 and 1. Do not include any explanation or additional text.
