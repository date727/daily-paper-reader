Abstract
Most adversarial attacks on deep reinforcement learning
(DRL) assume white-box access to the victim policy, which
rarely holds in practice. This paper studies transfer-based
black-box attacks on DRL: the attacker crafts observation per-
turbations on a white-box surrogate agent and feeds them to an
unknown victim. We formulate the attack as return minimiza-
tion under a per-step perturbation budget. We first show that
transplanting transferable image-classification attacks (FGSM,
MI-FGSM, and NI-FGSM) with a per-step objective yields
perturbations that transfer but are no stronger than random
noise of the same budget. We then propose a trajectory-level
attack that optimizes a sequence of perturbations over a reced-
ing horizon through a differentiable model of the environment
and a temperature-smoothed surrogate policy, with the same
optimizers. On CartPole-v1 with ten DQN and DDQN agents
and 100 surrogate–victim pairs, the trajectory-level attack out-
performs per-step attacks and random noise in the white-box,
cross-model, and cross-algorithm settings.
1
Introduction
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has achieved remarkable
success in learning complex control tasks and adapting to
dynamic environments [12, 26, 29]. However, DRL policies
inherit the adversarial fragility of deep neural networks: small,
carefully crafted perturbations of the observations can steer an
agent into catastrophic actions [11, 24, 34]. Such vulnerabili-
ties have been demonstrated across modalities and tasks, in-
cluding face recognition [20], speech recognition [8], dialogue
generation [19], the efficiency of dynamic neural networks and
large language models [4, 5], backdoors in vision-language
models [6], and policy manipulation in multi-robot reinforce-
ment learning [21, 27].
Most attacks on DRL assume white-box access to the vic-
tim’s parameters [11, 24, 30, 34]. In practice, a deployed

policy is usually inaccessible, so the attacker has to operate
in a black-box setting [11, 28]. A practical strategy is to craft
transferable adversarial examples on a white-box surrogate
model and apply them to the victim. This strategy has proven
highly effective for image classification [7, 20, 23] and speech
recognition [8], and has also been demonstrated in DRL across
policies and algorithms [11, 28]. In particular, prior work iden-
tifies shared adversarial features across MDPs [13] and shows
that adversarial training can leave policies vulnerable to natu-
ral, black-box perturbations [14]. Building on this evidence,
we study whether explicitly optimizing future state trajectories
improves transfer over per-step attack objectives.
A natural first step is to apply strong transferable attacks
from image classification independently at every time step.
We find that, on CartPole, such perturbations transfer across
seeds and algorithms but are no more damaging than random
noise at the corners of the same budget. The per-step objective
is myopic: it only targets the current action, whereas a well-
trained policy quickly corrects a single wrong action.
We therefore lift the attack objective from single actions to
trajectories. Our attack optimizes a sequence of perturbations
over a receding horizon so that the surrogate agent, rolled
out through a differentiable environment model, is driven
toward failure. We solve this problem with the momentum
and Nesterov optimizers of MI-FGSM and NI-FGSM, and
we smooth the surrogate policy with a temperature to avoid
overfitting to its exact decision boundary.
Our contributions are: (1) a return-minimization formu-
lation of transfer attacks on DRL that explains why per-
step relaxations fall short (§3.1); (2) a trajectory-level at-
tack that unifies FGSM, MI-FGSM, and NI-FGSM under a
receding-horizon objective (§3.2); and (3) an evaluation of
white-box, cross-model, and cross-algorithm transfer over ten
DQN/DDQN agents and 100 surrogate–victim pairs (§4).

Related Work
Transferable adversarial attacks. Transferability can be
improved with iterative attacks [18, 25], momentum [7], Nes-
1

terov look-ahead [23], variance tuning [32], and input diver-
sity [33]. They have also been tailored to face recognition [20]
and speech recognition [8]. Beyond accuracy, adversarial
inputs can degrade model efficiency [4, 5], manipulate text
generation [19], or implant stealthy backdoors [6, 10].
Adversarial attacks on DRL. Huang et al. [11] first
showed that FGSM [9] perturbations degrade DRL policies
in Atari games [2] and briefly examined transfer across poli-
cies and algorithms. Lin et al. [24] proposed the strategically
timed attack and the enchanting attack, where the latter uses a
learned dynamics model to plan a sequence of target actions.
Optimal white-box adversaries can be learned with reinforce-
ment learning [30, 34], and domain randomization improves
transfer [28]. In multi-agent settings, PIMbot manipulates the
policies and incentives of robot teams [21, 27]. Korkmaz [13]
shows that sensitive directions can transfer across states and
MDPs, including to adversarially trained policies. Korkmaz
[14] further demonstrates that natural observation transforma-
tions can undermine nominally robust policies in a black-box
setting. Our focus is on optimizing temporally coordinated
perturbations through a differentiable dynamics model and
evaluating their transfer across seeds and algorithms.
Diagnosing and evaluating robustness. Korkmaz and
Brown-Cohen [17] detect adversarial directions using local
quadratic approximations of the policy loss. Korkmaz [15]
analyzes how unstable directions evolve across states and time,
revealing limitations of robust training. Korkmaz [16] shows
that enforcing local invariance can impair counterfactual value
learning. These findings motivate evaluating defenses beyond
the particular perturbations used during training; our experi-
ments examine attack effectiveness rather than establishing a
robustness guarantee.
Sequence-level objectives. In reinforcement learning for
language-model reasoning, SPEAR [22] uses order-aware
alignment of symbolic reasoning trajectories to provide pro-
cess rewards for distillation. The connection to our work is
the use of sequence structure in the objective; SPEAR stud-
ies reasoning distillation, whereas we optimize observation
perturbations for adversarial control.

Why the trajectory objective transfers. First, Eq. (4) de-
pends on the environment dynamics, which the surrogate and
the victim share, rather than on the surrogate’s exact action
at each step, so perturbations that push the system toward
failure over several steps help against any policy that reacts
similarly. Second, a larger temperature κ smooths the surro-
gate’s decision boundary, which differs between agents, and
yields gradients that follow the overall trend of the Q-function,
similar to the gradient stabilization of momentum [7].

Main Results
Per-step attacks transfer but are weak. Iterative per-step
attacks are clearly stronger than FGSM: in the white-box set-
ting at a budget of 0.1, MI-FGSM and NI-FGSM reduce the
DQN return from 500 to 159.5 and 153.5, whereas FGSM only
reaches 262.5 (Table 1, white-box). They transfer across seeds
almost without loss (147.7 from one DQN to another) but are
consistently weaker than Sign noise (74.3). Transfer across
algorithms is also asymmetric: per-step attacks crafted on
DDQN barely affect DQN victims, whose return stays above
320 (Table 1, cross-algorithm).
Trajectory-level attacks are much stronger. In the white-
box setting at a budget of 0.1, Traj-NI-FGSM reduces the
return to 18.5 on DQN and 13.9 on DDQN, 4–9× lower than
Sign noise. At the smaller budget of 0.05, where all per-step
attacks leave the victims above a return of 277, Traj-MI-FGSM
reaches 73.5 on DQN and 157.3 on DDQN.
Transfer across seeds and algorithms. For cross-model
transfer at a budget of 0.1, the trajectory attacks reduce DQN
and DDQN victims to 59.6 and 41.1, compared with 147.7
and 211.2 for the best per-step attack and 74.3 and 121.2
for Sign noise. They also remove the asymmetry of cross-
algorithm transfer: perturbations crafted on DDQN reduce
DQN victims to 53.1, compared with more than 320 for per-
step attacks. This supports our hypothesis that an objective
defined through the shared dynamics transfers better than one
defined through individual actions. At a budget of 0.05, they
remain the strongest in every column, with a smaller margin
(e.g., 190.0 compared with 240.5 for Sign noise).
Momentum and look-ahead. Momentum and look-ahead
help most in the white-box setting (56.5 for Traj-FGSM com-
pared with about 19 on DQN at a budget of 0.1). Under trans-
fer, the three trajectory operators perform similarly, so the
trajectory objective itself is the main source of transferability.
5
Conclusion
Per-step transfer attacks from image classification transfer
across DRL agents but are no stronger than random corner
noise. Optimizing perturbation sequences over a receding
horizon through a differentiable environment model makes the
same optimizers far more effective in white-box, cross-model,
and cross-algorithm settings, which calls for defenses against
temporally coordinated perturbations. Extending the attack to
high-dimensional observations and learned dynamics models
is left for future work.
