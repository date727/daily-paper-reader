Anti-Persona: Disrupting Unauthorized Identity Binding and
Recognition in Personalized Vision–Language Models
Abhishek Basu∗1, Fahad Shamshad∗1, Karthik Nandakumar1, 2
1MBZUAI, UAE
2Michigan State University, USA
Few-shot personalization enables large vision–language models (LVLMs) to learn user-
specific visual concepts for applications such as personalized retrieval and subject-aware
querying. However, it also creates a privacy risk: an adversary can bind a target identity
from a few reference images and subsequently detect that identity in new images through
natural-language queries. We introduce Anti-Persona, an image-level defense against unau-
thorized identity binding and recognition in personalized LVLMs. Our key insight is that
identity personalization relies on visual features shared across multiple reference images.
We aggregate these features into an identity prototype and optimize visually subtle pertur-
bations that disrupt prototype alignment in the vision-encoder space. Spatial smoothing
and low-frequency preservation further promote visual fidelity and practical resilience to
image compression. The resulting protection does not depend on a specific prompt and
supports both proactive anti-personalization and reactive image protection. Experiments
on two representative personalized LVLMs demonstrate protection rates of up to 95.0%
while preserving visual fidelity. The method remains stable across prompt variations and
evaluated identity-query tasks, and improves black-box transfer under encoder mismatch.

Introduction
Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) have made significant progress in aligning visual and
textual representations [15, 12].
Trained on web-scale image-text corpora, they can answer
free-form questions, follow visual instructions, and associate language with fine-grained visual
concepts that previously required task-specific architectures [33]. These capabilities have enabled
applications such as accessibility tools, creative assistants, and consumer photo platforms [30].
An increasingly important capability of LVLMs is personalization, which adapts a model to a
user-specific visual concept from only a few reference images [19]. Given several images of a
person, a personalized LVLM can learn that individual as a new visual concept and subsequently
recognize them in held-out images. This is typically achieved by learning a compact subject-
specific representation on top of a frozen backbone [18, 1], enabling personalized assistance,
photo organization, identity-aware retrieval, and subject-specific querying [8].
While personalization benefits legitimate users, its low cost and few-shot data requirements also
make it accessible to adversaries. A few publicly available photos may be sufficient to bind a
person’s identity to an LVLM [25, 18, 1]. Once personalized, the model can be queried in natural
language to determine whether the target appears in new images [2]. This creates a significant
privacy risk: a general-purpose LVLM can be repurposed as a targeted identity recognizer without
the person’s knowledge or consent.
Unlike conventional face-recognition systems that require
dedicated pipelines or specialized models [36, 11], personalized LVLMs expose identity detection
through a flexible prompt-based interface, lowering the barrier to unauthorized use without
specialized expertise.
A growing line of work studies image-level protection against unauthorized use, primarily for
generative models. Anti-DreamBooth [26], Glaze [24], and PhotoGuard [23] apply visually con-
strained perturbations to interfere with image editing, style imitation, or subject-driven genera-

model fails to edit, mimic, or reproduce protected content [35]. Recognition-based personaliza-
tion poses a different challenge: the model need not synthesize the target identity, but only learn

In this paper, we introduce an image-level protection framework for recognition-based personal-
ization in LVLMs (see Figure 1). Our approach follows two key observations. First, personalized
LVLMs rely on vision-encoder representations as the visual evidence for identity binding. We
therefore optimize protective perturbations in the encoder space rather than attacking a partic-
ular prompt response. Second, identity binding relies on cues that remain stable across multiple
images of the same person, rather than on memorizing each image independently. Consequently,
perturbing an image only with respect to its own representation may leave shared identity cues
available for personalization. We address this limitation by aggregating multiple reference repre-
sentations into an identity prototype and optimizing each protected image to reduce its alignment
with this prototype. This directly targets identity-consistent structure rather than only image-
specific features.
In addition to the identity-disruption objective, we refine the perturbation to make the protection
effective under realistic image-sharing conditions. Images shared online are often recompressed,
which can attenuate high-frequency adversarial signals. We therefore encourage spatial coher-
ence to suppress abrupt local variations and concentrate perturbation energy in lower-frequency
components that are less susceptible to JPEG attenuation [27]. These refinements are designed
to improve perceptual quality and compression resilience while preserving the identity-disruption
objective. Because the objective operates on identity-relevant representations rather than a spe-
cific query, we evaluate it across prompt formulations. We additionally study transfer when the
protection encoder differs from that of the target personalized LVLM.

• We identify and formalize a privacy threat in few-shot personalized LVLMs, in which an

• We propose a multi-reference identity-disruption objective that aggregates encoder repre-

• We introduce edge-aware spatial smoothing and low-frequency preservation to reduce vis-

• We evaluate Anti-Persona on representative personalized LVLMs, covering both proactive

Related Work
Personalization in LVLMs.
Personalization has been extensively studied in generative models,
where methods such as DreamBooth [21], Textual Inversion [6], and Custom Diffusion [9] adapt
text-to-image models to synthesize a specific subject from a few reference images [34]. In contrast,
personalization in LVLMs is more recent and serves a different purpose: rather than generating
images of a subject, the model learns to recognize and interact with user-specific visual concepts
in downstream vision-language tasks [19]. Recent methods such as Yo’LLaVA [18] adopt token-
based personalization, where a learned identifier is bound to a target subject, while MyVLM [1]
introduces architectural components such as a dedicated concept head for personalized recogni-
tion. These approaches show that LVLMs can acquire identity-specific representations from only
a small number of examples, enabling applications such as personalized retrieval and subject-
aware querying.
However, this capability also exposes new vulnerabilities, as the same mech-
anisms that enable efficient personalization can be exploited to inject sensitive identities into
models without user consent.
Protection against Unauthorized Personalization. A growing body of work has explored adver-
sarial perturbations as a defense against unauthorized use of personal images by generative
AI systems [16]. General image protection methods such as PhotoGuard [23] and Glaze [24]
perturb images to resist malicious image editing or style imitation [31], while methods such
as Anti-DreamBooth [26], MIST [13], Anti-Diffusion [38], and AdvDM [14] specifically target
subject-driven personalization in diffusion models. These approaches are designed for synthesis-
based threats, where protection is achieved by preventing a generative model from faithfully
editing, mimicking, or reproducing protected visual content. Recognition-based personalization
in LVLMs presents a different defense objective. The personalized model need not synthesize the
target identity; it only needs to learn a discriminative representation that supports prompt-based
identity detection. As a result, defenses designed for generative misuse are not directly optimized
to prevent identity binding or subsequent recognition in personalized LVLMs. In this work, we
address this gap by formulating image-level protection for recognition-based personalization, where

Preliminaries
3.1
Personalization in LVLMs
A large vision–language model (LVLM) can be represented as a vision encoder followed by a
language model. Given an image x ∈RH×W×3, a frozen vision encoder fϕ produces a visual
representation fϕ(x) ∈Rd, where any visual projection module is absorbed into fϕ. Given a
user query q, the language model produces a response as gθ(fϕ(x), q). Although this model can
answer general visual questions, it does not explicitly bind new user-specific identities absent
from its training data.
Personalization adapts the LVLM to a subject s using a small reference set Rs = {x1, x2, . . . , xn},
whose images depict the same individual under different visual conditions. The systems consid-
ered in this work keep the pretrained vision–language backbone frozen and learn lightweight
subject-specific parameters ψs from Rs.
After personalization, the response to a test image
xtest and query q depends on the test-image representation fϕ(xtest) and ψs. These parameters
may be implemented as identifier tokens, lightweight prediction heads, or related personalization
modules.
Representative personalized LVLMs instantiate ψs differently. Yo’LLaVA [18] learns latent identi-
fier tokens, including a subject token such as ⟨sks⟩, using conversational supervision. MyVLM [1]
instead trains a lightweight concept head hψs : Rd →R using positive target images and negative
or distractor images. Despite these architectural differences, both methods learn subject-specific
parameters from encoder representations, making the vision encoder a common interface for iden-
tity personalization. Consequently, if protected images no longer produce a consistent identity
signal in the encoder space, the personalization mechanism receives weaker evidence for binding or
recognizing the target subject. We therefore formulate protection at the encoder-representation
level rather than against a specific prompt, response, or personalization head, allowing the de-
fense to target identity evidence shared across different personalized LVLM designs.
3.2
Threat Model
We study unauthorized identity recognition using personalized LVLMs. Let s denote the target
individual. The adversary seeks to determine whether s appears in a query image, whereas the
defender releases protected images ˜x = x + δ that disrupt identity binding or recognition while
remaining visually similar to x.
Adversary goals and capabilities. Given reference images of s, the adversary follows the standard
personalization pipeline of the target LVLM: the pretrained vision–language backbone remains
frozen, and subject-specific parameters ψs are learned from the available references. The adver-
sary then uses natural-language prompts to determine whether s appears in new images and
may vary the formulation of these prompts. We consider a non-adaptive adversary using an off-
the-shelf personalized LVLM; modifying the personalization algorithm, purification, adversarial
training, and explicit perturbation removal are outside our scope.
Defender goals and capabilities.
The defender aims to prevent reliable recognition of s while
preserving visual fidelity. The defender uses the user’s own reference images to estimate the
identity prototype but does not assume knowledge of the adversary’s prompts. In the gray-box
setting, the defender has access to the target vision encoder fϕ. In the black-box setting, the
defender has no access to the target encoder’s parameters or gradients and instead optimizes
using a surrogate encoder fϕ′.

Method

protected image ˜x = x + δ is difficult to associate with the target identity s by a personalized
LVLM. We formulate protection in the representation space of the vision encoder, since this
is the shared interface through which visual identity information is provided to downstream
personalization modules. The design follows three principles.
First, the perturbation should
disrupt identity-relevant encoder features before they are used for personalization or recognition.
Second, because identity personalization is learned from multiple reference images, protection
should target the identity-consistent structure shared across those images rather than only image-
specific features.

4.1
Identity Prototype
A naive way to perturb an image is to move its vision encoder representation away from its clean
embedding, for example by reducing the similarity between fϕ(x + δ) and fϕ(x). This instance-
level objective can suppress features of the individual image, but it does not explicitly target
the information that supports few-shot identity personalization. In this setting, the personalized
model observes multiple reference images of the same individual and can rely on cues that
remain stable across variations in pose, illumination, expression, and background. As a result,
perturbing each image only with respect to its own embedding may leave the shared identity
signal sufficiently intact for the model to bind or recognize the subject.
To target this shared signal, we define an identity prototype µs from the user’s reference set
Rs = {x1, x2, . . . , xn}. Let zi = fϕ(xi) denote the encoder representation of the i-th reference
image. We compute the prototype as the centroid of the reference embeddings:

4.3
Perturbation Refinement
The objective in Eq. 5 disrupts identity representations, but it does not control the structure of
the perturbation in image space. This can limit practical deployment in two ways. First, an ℓ∞
constraint bounds the perturbation magnitude but does not prevent locally irregular patterns that
may become perceptible. Second, adversarial signals concentrated in high-frequency components
can be weakened by lossy image compression, which is common in online sharing pipelines. We
address these issues with two refinements applied after each optimization step.
Spatial smoothing. To reduce perceptible artifacts, we regularize the perturbation by suppressing
abrupt local variations. Specifically, we apply Gaussian smoothing G(·) using a Sobel-derived
mask Medge computed from the clean image:
δ ←G(δ) ⊙Medge + δ ⊙(1 −Medge).
7


This operation encourages spatial coherence in visually sensitive regions while preserving the
overall direction of the optimized perturbation. As a result, the perturbation remains aligned
with the identity-disruption objective while reducing local artifacts that may otherwise be visible.
Low-frequency preservation. To improve robustness to compression, we further constrain the
perturbation to retain low-frequency structure. After each update, we apply a patch-wise dis-
crete cosine transform (DCT) to δ, keep coefficients selected by a low-pass mask, and reconstruct
the perturbation using the inverse DCT. This concentrates perturbation energy in spectral com-
ponents that are more likely to survive JPEG compression, rather than in high-frequency com-
ponents that are easily attenuated. The refinement therefore improves compression robustness
while preserving the identity-disruption objective.

Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setup
We evaluate whether the proposed protection reduces unauthorized identity recognition while
preserving visual fidelity. Our experiments cover reactive and proactive protection, two personal-
ized LVLM architectures, prompt and query variation, black-box encoder transfer, and sensitivity
to the number of reference images.
Implementation details. We implement the method in PyTorch and generate protected images
at 336 × 336 resolution. Unless otherwise stated, pixel-space perturbations use an ℓ∞budget
of ϵ = 4/255 and are clipped to the valid image range after every update. Spatial smoothing
uses a Gaussian kernel with (σ, k) = (0.8, 5). In the gray-box setting, protection is optimized
using the victim-aligned OpenAI CLIP ViT-L/14@336 encoder [20]. In the black-box setting,
this encoder is never used. Instead, we form a six-encoder surrogate pool comprising CLIP-B/16,
CLIP-L/14@224, CLIPA-L/14@336, SigLIP [32], ConvNeXtV2 [29], and supervised ViT-L. Each
leave-one-surrogate-out configuration optimizes over the remaining five encoders and is evaluated
on Yo’LLaVA. Experiments use a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 (48GB) GPU.
Dataset and personalized LVLMs. We use the personalized identity-recognition benchmark from
Yo’LLaVA [18], which contains ten identities with approximately 15–20 images per identity.
Following the benchmark protocol, 5–10 images are used as personalization references and the
remaining valid images are held out for evaluation. We evaluate Yo’LLaVA [18], which learns
subject-specific identifier tokens, and MyVLM [1], which trains a lightweight concept head over
frozen visual features. These systems represent two distinct encoder-based personalization mech-
anisms.
Baselines. We report an unprotected (Clean) reference and compare against two baseline families.
First, we consider five ℓ∞-bounded pixel-space attacks: FGSM [7], I-FGSM [10], MI-FGSM [4],
TI-FGSM [5], and PGD [17].
Each baseline optimizes an instance-level feature-repulsion ob-
jective that reduces the similarity between fϕ(˜x) and fϕ(x), providing a controlled comparison
with conventional feature-space perturbations. These baselines use the same perturbation bud-
get and encoder-access regime as ours. For broader comparison, we also report DiffAttack [3]
and WDP [22], two unrestricted diffusion-based references. Because they are not ℓ∞-bounded,
we distinguish them from equal-budget comparisons and report their visual fidelity.
Within
each protocol, all evaluated methods use the same identities, personalized Large VLMs, image
resolution, and text prompts.
Evaluation protocols. We evaluate protection under two protocols corresponding to the threat
model. Following Sec. 3.2, the reactive protocol personalizes the LVLM using clean references
and evaluates it on protected held-out images. The proactive protocol instead personalizes the
model using protected references and evaluates it on clean held-out images. More details are

Conclusion
We introduced an image-level defense against unauthorized identity recognition in personalized
LVLMs. Anti-Persona aggregates multiple reference embeddings into an identity prototype and
optimizes protected images to disrupt identity-consistent encoder features. Spatial and frequency-
domain refinements are designed to preserve visual quality and improve resilience to image
compression. Experiments show effective reactive protection on Yo’LLaVA and MyVLM and
proactive protection on Yo’LLaVA, while maintaining high visual fidelity. The method remains
stable across prompt formulations, extends to VQA-style identity queries, and improves black-
box transfer under encoder mismatch. These results motivate stronger privacy safeguards for
personalized multimodal systems.
