Abstract. We evaluate whether the improvements adver-tised for ChatGPT Images 2.5 translate into better perfor-mance on forgery tasks with predetermined answers. We com-pare its Flare and Sunburst API models with GPT-Image-2 re-run in the same week, using receipt-field edits, repeated edit-ing, product placement and fine-print rendering. After image registration, Flare and Sunburst show fewer OCR-detected changes to surrounding receipt text (31.7% and 31.2% versus 44.2% for both GPT-Image-2 baselines), mainly on CORD receipts, without a detectable improvement in target-field cor-rectness. Flare retains fewer earlier edits on CORD receipts, while photo-edit sequences provide little separation between models. Product codes are more often legible with Images 2.5, alongside larger product placement; the analyses do not es-tablish a fidelity gain independent of size. Fine-print improve-ments remain unresolved below the OCR reliability limit. Re-fusals are rare and localisation is weak in both generations. At a fixed detection threshold, Community Forensics flags 68.6% of controlled Images 2.5 images averaged across cells, versus 35.9% of self-reported images posted online. These results motivate task-specific evaluation of advertised capabilities and defences, with explicit limits on what automatic checks can establish. 

## 1 Introduction 

Image generators are marketed as creative tools, but the same editing abilities serve document and marketplace fraud. En-trust’s 2025 Identity Fraud Report records a 244% year-over-year rise in digital document forgeries, with digital tampering now more common than physical counterfeiting [5]. Our own earlier study (AIForge-Doc v2, 3,066 receipt and form forgeries made with GPT-Image-2) found such forgeries essen-tially invisible to the eye: shown a real and a forged document side by side, human inspectors picked out the forgery 0.501 of the time, which is chance level. Three automated detectors did little better (AUC 0.532–0.599), although two of them score 0.962 and 0.852 on conventional tampering [31]. When a vendor releases a new model advertising better editing, fraud and trust-and-safety teams need to know two things: does it make forgery easier, and do existing defences still work? 

Why existing evaluations fall short. Neither of the evalua-tions usually published with a release answers these questions. Public leaderboards rank models by pairwise human prefer-ence votes [1,3], and a vendor’s system card reports how often outputs are unsafe or blocked [15]. Neither reports whether a forged receipt field is correct while surrounding text is pre-served. The system card includes answer-graded biological and cyber capability tests, but not these forgery outcomes. Forensic benchmarks, including our own, ask the complemen-tary question of whether a forgery is caught, and so measure the effect of a capability rather than the capability itself. Our earlier study also left open why its forgeries were so hard to catch: it tested one model at one quality tier, so the cause could be the model, the quality tier, or our benchmark. RQ3 and RQ4 below compare generations, tiers and evaluation samples without identifying a causal explanation. 

What OpenAI claims. On 8 September 2026 OpenAI re-leased ChatGPT Images 2.5 as two API models, Flare and Sunburst, advertising four improvements over GPT-Image-2 [16]: precise local editing (one element changes, the rest holds), multi-turn consistency (earlier edits survive later ones), 

reference fidelity (a referenced product or person keeps its features in a new setting) and sharper detail (two new quality tiers, xhigh and max , and 4K output). Flare is also adver-tised as faster. For a fraudster these translate into four abilities: changing one field on a receipt, editing the same piece of ev-idence repeatedly, placing a real product into a fake listing, and producing convincing small print. We build one test per ability whose correct result is known in advance (Figure 1) and compare both new models with GPT-Image-2, re-run in the same week at a cheaper and at a more expensive quality tier (§3.1). 

Research questions. RQ1. Capability. On a forgery task with a known correct answer, does Images 2.5 do each advertised thing better than GPT-Image-2? Product-code legibility improves alongside larger placement; independent fidelity gains and finer-print improvements remain unresolved (§4.1– §4.4).

## 2 Related Work 

Tampering corpora and the AIForge-Doc releases. 

Document-forensics corpora pair tampered images with Evaluating ChatGPT Images 2.5 on Forgery Tasks 3

masks. DocTamper [22] supplies 170,000 document images with a document-specific detector, and OSTF [23] extends the question to scene text altered by eight text-editing models, testing generalisation to unseen forgery types. AIForge-Doc v1 [32] paired 4,061 diffusion forgeries of financial and form documents with pixel-precise masks; v2 [31] held those speci-fications fixed, swapped the generator for GPT-Image-2 and brought human accuracy in a two-alternative forced choice to 0.501, which is chance level. The v3 release keeps the cata-logue and source corpora and swaps the generator again. The earlier releases emphasized detection; this study adds checks of requested-edit correctness and content preservation. 

Preference and ground-truth capability evaluation. 

Blind-vote arenas aggregate pairwise human preferences into Elo-style ratings [1, 3]. This is the main public evidence for Images 2.5, but a preference vote cannot tell whether a forged total is correct. Editing benchmarks delegate judge-ment to a model: GEditBench v2 [11] trains a pairwise visual-consistency judge on human preferences, and ImgEdit-Bench [33] has GPT-4o rate instruction adherence, editing quality and detail preservation on a five-point scale, although model evaluators favour their own generations [19]. Text-rendering evaluations come closest to checking against ground truth: MARIO-Eval [2] and the AnyText benchmark [30] read generated text back with OCR, using protocols for visual text accuracy. AnyText also supports text editing; our contribution is the combination of field correctness, collateral preservation and defence measurements on forgery tasks. Subject-fidelity metrics such as DINO and CLIP-I [27] score embedding sim-ilarity to a reference; they reward object identity and do not check whether small label text is reproduced. 

Public detectors. TruFor [7] fuses the RGB image with a learned noise fingerprint to give a localisation map and an integrity score; DocTamper is the public document-specific localiser evaluated here; and Community Forensics [20] is a generator-agnostic detector trained on images from thousands of generators. As in our earlier study we omit reconstruction-based AEROBLADE [25], which is unsuited to local edits because most of the image is untouched. Our tasks connect the release claims to checkable forgery outcomes and public-detector measurements. 

## 3 Evaluation Design 

Several primary contrasts and the statistical framework were chosen after results were known; §3.5 documents these deci-sions. 

3.1 Models and baselines 

We compare the Images 2.5 models Flare and Sunburst (gpt-image-2.5-flare ,

gpt-image-2.5-sunburst ) with the previous model, GPT-Image-2. AIForge-Doc is our forgery dataset: v1 and v2 are earlier releases [31, 32] and v3 is this paper’s, so these are dataset versions, not model versions. For v3 we re-ran GPT-Image-2 in the same week as the Images 2.5 models (September 2026). 

Two baselines. E1–E3 run both Images 2.5 models at the 

medium quality tier, OpenAI’s quality parameter being what fixes an image’s price, and compare them with GPT-Image-2 at low , the nearest cheaper tier, and at medium ,which costs four times as much: the two baselines bracket the Images 2.5 setting in price, and no GPT-Image-2 tier costs the same. E4 and the paired timings instead compare settings of equal cost, so E4 compares GPT-Image-2 medium with Images 2.5 high and GPT-Image-2 high with Images 2.5 

max (Appendix A). E1 ran both baselines from the start. In E2 and E3 the low baseline was added after the main runs, with identical stimuli, prompts, masks, edit orders and seeds, under an addendum fixed before any of its images existed and with scoring rules unchanged; its images are about 11 hours younger than those of the configurations they are compared with (§3.5). Both contrasts enter the claim’s test family. All calls went through OpenAI’s API from one account and were logged, including refusals and failures; Appendix B gives the setup and the log. 

3.2 What we measure: capability, review check and de-fence 

Each advertised improvement becomes a forgery task whose correct result is fixed before any image is generated (Figure 1). We score three things, labelled L1–L3 in the figures and tables and named in words everywhere else. Capability (L1): did the model make the requested edit correctly? Review check (L2): an automated proxy for review-relevant preservation of other text and earlier edits. It does not measure human acceptance of a forgery. Defence (L3): does OpenAI refuse the request, and can public tools detect the image or localise the edited region? We also report speed, which OpenAI advertises for Flare (§5.3). 

How outputs are checked. Capability and review checks use instruments whose errors are measurable against the cor-rect answer: OCR (easyocr [10]); SIFT [12] or ORB [26] geometry with RANSAC [6]; colour distance ( ∆E00 [28]); and open-vocabulary detection (OWLv2 [13]) with a colour test. We do not use image-embedding similarity (CLIP [24], DINOv2 [18]): it tells whether the object is the same kind of thing, not whether its label text is correct. Detectors enter only for the defence questions, and only public ones: Doc-Tamper [22], TruFor [7] and Community Forensics [20]. No OpenAI model judges OpenAI output [19]; in one E2 variant an OpenAI text model ( gpt-5.4-nano ) passes our instruc-tions to the image model but does no scoring. Earlier scorer versions were spot-checked on blind sheets by an AI coding agent (Claude), not human raters; the final registered E1 token set was not independently validated. Agreement counts carry no inferential weight.

The OCR ceiling is the smallest glyph size OCR reads reliably on clean renders (9 px cap height for E4; per output for E3); lines below it are not counted against the model. A pixel-level measure of change outside the edited region is reported in Appendix D.5. 

3.3 Four forgery tasks E1: Change one field on a receipt. AIForge-Doc specifica-tions on CORD [21] and WildReceipt [29], filtered on crop aspect ratio (Appendix D.2), are run as masked field edits with identical prompt and alpha mask on every model, scored on the 199 specifications that every model rendered. Capabil-ity: field OCR equals the forged value, where OCR reads the source field. Review proxy: OCR detects a change in other text in the crop. Outputs can shift slightly (median 2.53 px for GPT-Image-2 medium , 0.87–1.08 px for Images 2.5), so we align each output to its source before re-reading the text; OCR alone flags a change on 12.1% of aligned unedited calibration copies. Strata by shift are a sensitivity. Defence: DocTam-per localisation, TruFor as a control for the paste seam, and Community Forensics on whole images (Appendix D). 

E2: Edit the same image repeatedly. E2a edits 60 receipts (30 CORD, 30 WildReceipt) 4 times, one field per turn in a seeded order, each turn sent as a fresh request. Capability: the exact target on its line at its own turn (success) and at the last turn (still intact); review check: never-edited fields unchanged. A joint endpoint counting every requested edit, not only the successful ones, was added post hoc as co-primary. E2b 

starts from 23 photographs without people (39 edit sequences) and adds one checkable element per turn, either a short code (checked by OCR) or a coloured object (checked by an object detector). Each turn is sent either as a fresh request or within one chat conversation, in which a text model relays each instruction. Capability: a successful edit is present anywhere at the last turn; review check: it is still in the right position (box overlap at least 0.3) (Appendix E). 

E3: Place a given product in a fake listing. 119 synthetic product images carry a nonsense brand, price, small SKU code and two-colour logo as ground truth, so no generator has a home-field advantage. Each model inserts every reference as a front-on hero shot and in-scene among other items; the label is warped back for exact OCR of brand, SKU and price, logo colour distance and logo shape overlap. Because a larger product yields larger glyphs, we also measure label area and relative glyph size, and compare models at matched glyph height and matched product size (exploratory; Appendix F). 

E4: Render small print. Each of 40 prompts asks for 6 exact random codes printed ever smaller, on every tier of every model and at 4K. Capability: the share of lines read exactly above the OCR ceiling, per prompt; prompts with no line above it in either configuration drop out and are counted. An intention-to-treat rate and a matched-glyph-height contrast are exploratory. Latency comes from prompt-paired synchronous runs on the E1 edits and on the photoreal core, a set of photo-realistic generations at every tier (Appendix G, J). 

3.4 Statistical analysis Tests and intervals. Contrasts are paired, Images 2.5 minus a baseline on the same units, clustered by specification (E1), receipt (E2a), source photo (E2b), reference (E3) or prompt (E4, latency). Each p-value comes from a two-sided cluster sign-flip permutation test with 20,000 permutations. Intervals are unadjusted percentile bootstrap 95% CIs over 2,000 cluster resamples. Exact McNemar tests are a sensitivity for E1. Nulls carry a minimum detectable difference where one can be computed, and readings of no difference in the defence results carry equivalence bounds (Appendix C). 

Correcting for multiple tests. Holm’s correction [8] is applied within each advertised claim, across layers, models and baselines: local editing , E1 target read and other text changed (8 tests); multi-turn consistency , E2a success, still in-tact, never-edited fields and the joint endpoint per corpus, with E2b survival and position (40); reference fidelity , E3 in-scene SKU exactness (4); and sharper detail , E4 exact lines at the two highest matched settings (4). A stricter Holm correction over all 56 primary tests is reported as a check. All other analyses are exploratory and do not determine the primary ver-dicts. The E3 (programmatic and rendered reference sets) and E4 sensitivity analyses carry their own Holm correction in 24 families; other exploratory analyses are reported unadjusted. 

3.5 Analysis plan and decision timeline 

The E1–E4 metrics were written into a dated design document on 10 September 2026, before E1 was scored and E2–E4 were generated. Within E1, E3 and E4 the primary contrast was chosen after the results were available, and the statistics framework above was adopted with every result known, so both are post hoc. The E2 rules were fixed before any full-run E2a estimate existed, but after the chat-mode E2b data had been scored in full, which is why those contrasts are exploratory, and after a pilot run had shown Flare’s numbers. Appendix C.1 gives the full sequence, with times and the documents that record them.

4.1 Precise local editing: does one edit leave the rest alone? Does registered OCR detect fewer collateral text changes? Yes. After each output is aligned with its source receipt, OCR detects changes to surrounding text less often with Images 2.5: 31.7% (Flare) and 31.2% (Sunburst) of 199 receipt edits, against 44.2% (GPT-Image-2 low ) and 44.2% ( medium ), which reach the same rate on different receipts. OCR alone reports a change on 12.1% of unedited receipts, providing a calibration reference rather than a guaranteed lower bound. All four differences ( −12 .6% to −13 .1%) are significant after Holm correction ( p ≤ 0.0075), and exact McNemar tests agree. 

Caveats. The reduction comes from CORD receipts ( −19 .0%to −25 .0%); WildReceipt shows no difference. It is also frag-ile: on outputs shifted by at most 1 px the differences are similar ( −14 .7% to −17 .9%) but significant, before correc-tion, only against low , and without aligning the outputs none is. Under the stricter correction across all primary tests, Flare against medium is not significant ( p = 0.066). Receipts were limited to the shapes the edit API accepts (Appendices D.4, D.3 and D.1). 

Is the forged value written correctly more often? No detectable change. On the 96 receipts where OCR can read the original field, every model writes the forged value cor-rectly in 67.7%–77.1% of edits, with no significant difference. The largest, Sunburst against low , is +9 .4% [ 0.0%, +19 .8%] (p = 0.44). The study could detect a difference of 14–15 pp (at 80% power), so smaller gains cannot be ruled out. Verdict: 

partly. Registered OCR detects fewer collateral text changes, mainly on CORD receipts and with sensitivity to small shifts; this endpoint lacks independent validation. Target-field cor-rectness does not improve detectably. 

4.2 Multi-turn consistency: do earlier edits survive later ones? Repeated edits to one receipt (E2a): Flare is worse. We edited one field per turn for 4 turns. On CORD receipts, Flare lost more of its earlier edits than GPT-Image-2 at either setting. Of Flare’s successful edits, 89% were still intact at the last turn, against 94% ( low ) and 98% ( medium ). The differences are −7% [ −14 %, −2%] and −9% [ −16 %, −3%] (Holm p = 0.021 and 0.002; both also pass the stricter all-test correction). Counting every requested edit, not just the successful ones, the share that both succeeded and lasted to the last turn is −22 % and −28 % lower for Flare (Holm p = 0.033 and 0.0039). 4.3 Reference fidelity: is a reference product copied faith-fully? Product codes are more legible, alongside larger place-ment. When a product is placed into a cluttered scene, OCR reads its small SKU code exactly more than twice as often on Images 2.5 outputs: 64% (Flare) and 61% (Sunburst) of 119 synthetic references, against 28% ( low ) and 23% ( medium ). All four differences survive both Holm corrections. For a fraudster this is a real gain, since a fake listing shows a read-able code more often. This does not establish more faithful copying independent of product size. Asked for a product about a tenth of the frame wide, Images 2.5 draws its label larger (4.1% and 3.9% of the frame, against 2.7% and 2.4%), while the letters are the same size relative to the label for every model. Because the model itself chooses how large to draw the product, comparing same-size products cannot separate fidelity from size; those contrasts are exploratory and none of them establishes a fidelity gain. Logo-shape overlap is mixed (Appendix F). On 117 physically rendered references, an exploratory real-ism check outside every primary family, the pattern replicates: in-scene SKU codes are read exactly on 69% (Flare) and 69% (Sunburst) of outputs against 43% ( low ) and 34% ( medium )(Flare +26 % and Sunburst +26 % against low ; Holm p within the set’s own family 2.0 × 10 −4), and Images 2.5 again places the label larger (4.0% and 3.7% of the frame, against 2.7% and 2.6%). 4.4 Sharper detail: is fine print more legible? Is fine print sharper? No measurable difference in the range OCR can read. We printed random codes at shrinking sizes. OCR reads reliably only text with a cap height above 9 px, and that limit sits above the steep part of the legibility curve, so what we can score is its flat top (the height-by-height breakdown is in Appendix G). That flat top is not a ceiling: settings there run from 78% to 95%, leaving room for a dif-ference to show. Above the limit, no difference is significant. The closest, Flare max against GPT-Image-2 high (the top setting of each), reads +13 .2% [ +4 .1%, +24 .0%] more lines exactly (Holm p = 0.054; 0.54 under the stricter correction). The comparison is uneven: it excludes 6 prompts (5 on the GPT-Image-2 side), and the two models are scored on different lines, because 71% of GPT-Image-2 high lines fall below the limit against 30% of Flare’s. Scoring unreadably small lines as failures would give +44 %, but that reflects the OCR limit, not the models. Within each model, no higher quality tier beats 

low on OCR-readable text (0 of 13 comparisons significant), and a height-by-height scan finds no band, above or below the limit, in which a higher tier reads better (Appendix G). 

Verdict: no measurable gain in text large enough for OCR to read; the sizes at which fine print actually becomes illegible are below what the instrument can score, and are untested. 

## 5 Detection, Localisation and Refusal 

We ask whether the usual defences still work on Images 2.5: does the model refuse the forgery request, can a forensic tool point to the edited region, and does a detector flag the image (Figure 3)? Each test also runs GPT-Image-2 in the same week, so it shows whether a defence changed with the new model, though not why. 

5.1 Refusal: both models rarely refuse 

Images 2.5 refused 7 of 2,000 masked receipt edits on first pass (0.35% [0.17%, 0.72%]). GPT-Image-2, sent the same request, prompt and alpha mask, refused 1 of 400 (0.25%). Both are far below the ∼10% refusal rate we measured for GPT-Image-2 on the same kind of request in our earlier study [31], so the drop is not specific to the new model. Paired on the 200 specifications every model was sent, Flare refused 1, Sunburst 0, and GPT-Image-2 1 ( low ) and 0 ( medium ). With so few refusals the models cannot be told apart (every p = 1; a difference would need at least 6 specifications refused by one model and not the other). The drop since our earlier study may reflect our changed request format as much as the date (Appendix H). 

5.2 Localisation: forensic tools barely find the edit 

DocTamper’s mean per-image localisation AUC is 0.588 [0.576, 0.600] on Flare and 0.599 [0.586, 0.612] on Sunburst, against 0.613 and 0.615 on GPT-Image-2 low and medium :weak for every model (AUC about 0.6, where 0.5 is chance), with confidence intervals above chance (Appendix I.3). Doc-Tamper localises Flare’s edits slightly less well than those of GPT-Image-2 medium (−0.025 [−0.048 , −0.002 ]), so Flare is slightly harder to localise; all model-to-model differences are within ±0.044 AUC (margins and tests, Appendix I.3). These results are for our pipeline, which edits a crop and pastes it back into the page; a forger who edits the whole page would leave no such boundary. TruFor’s much higher scores (AUC 0.91–0.92) come from that paste-back boundary, not from the edit itself, and it could score only pages up to 2,772 px on the long edge (Appendix I.2). Community Foren-sics has no localisation map. Localisation across our three dataset releases is in Appendix I.1.

5.4 Detection: benchmark images versus images posted online 

The tested quality tiers show no clear pattern in detectability: Community Forensics’ AUC stays between 0.88 and 0.94 across every 1024-px setting, although we cannot show it is flat (Appendix J). At a 5% false-positive rate set on 364 authentic images, Community Forensics flags, on average, 68.6% of images across 10 controlled Images 2.5 cells (unweighted mean; 95% bootstrap interval over cells 64.7%–72.7%, range 58.0%– 80.0%). On 1,291 images posted online whose authors iden-tify them as Images 2.5 it flags 35.9% (Wilson 95% interval 33.4%–38.6%): benchmark detection is higher than detection on images posted online. The comparison is observational. Membership rests on self-report, because platforms strip C2PA manifests. The intervals treat images as independent (posts are not clustered by author) and the threshold as fixed (its uncertainty is not propagated). Posted PNGs are detected at 42.9% (35.6%–50.6%) and JPEGs at 34.9% (32.2%–37.8%); the PNGs are not content-matched (Appendix L). 

## 6 Discussion 

6.1 Measured improvements and their limits 

Two measured outcomes improve with Images 2.5: product codes are more often legible, and registered OCR detects fewer collateral text changes in single receipt edits. The for-mer accompanies larger product placement; post hoc size matching does not establish a fidelity gain independent of placement. The latter is concentrated on CORD receipts and depends on registration and token selection. Without indepen-dent validation of the final registered endpoint, it remains an instrument-defined result rather than evidence of human ac-ceptance. Preference rankings do not directly measure either endpoint. The defence outcomes require separate interpretations. Both same-week generations rarely refused the tested receipt ed-its. DocTamper localisation was weak for both, with a small paired difference between Flare and GPT-Image-2 medium .Quality-tier comparisons showed no clear pattern in Com-munity Forensics detection, but did not establish equivalence across the tested settings. These comparisons do not identify why refusal was lower than in our earlier study: request format, route and sampling also changed. Nor do separate capabil-ity and defence measurements establish whether improved editing causes a change in detectability. At the evaluated threshold, detection was lower on the self-reported posted-image sample than the controlled Images 2.5 cells. This observational gap motivates evaluation on deploy-ment traffic; it does not establish a general benchmark bias or isolate the effects of content, format and provenance. It concerns whole-image detection, not the transfer of document-localisation results. 

6.2 Recommendations For fraud and trust-and-safety teams. Do not assume provider refusal will block these receipt edits. Treat forensic heat maps cautiously: the tested document localiser weakly separates edited pixels, and TruFor can respond to the bound-ary of a pasted crop. Combine image checks with indepen-dently verifiable information, such as issuer records and arith-metic consistency. Our experiments did not evaluate the effec-tiveness of those operational checks. Do not assume a higher quality tier will reliably evade the tested detector. Validate performance on images representative of your own traffic. 

For model vendors. Complement preference ratings with Evaluating ChatGPT Images 2.5 on Forgery Tasks 9

tests whose correct answers are fixed in advance. Compare previous and new models on matched tasks, identifying both quality-tier labels and billed cost. Report refusal rates for named misuse types with contemporaneous controls, and make provenance metadata identify the generating model. Appendix M gives the measurement and design limitations. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We evaluated four improvements advertised for ChatGPT Im-ages 2.5 using forgery tasks with predetermined answers. Af-ter registration, OCR detected fewer collateral receipt-text changes with Flare and Sunburst than with the GPT-Image-2 baselines, mainly on CORD receipts; the final registered end-point lacks independent validation. Product codes were more often legible alongside larger placement, without establishing a fidelity gain independent of size. Flare retained fewer earlier edits on CORD receipts, photo-edit sequences offered little separation, and finer-print improvements remain unresolved below the OCR limit. Both generations rarely refused the tested receipt edits and were weakly localised, with small measured differences; detection equivalence across quality tiers was not established. Community Forensics flagged fewer self-reported posted images than controlled images at the eval-uated threshold (35.9% versus a 68.6% mean across cells). These findings support evaluating requested-edit correctness, preservation proxies and defence performance separately, with claims bounded by the tasks and instruments used.

## D E1: Alter One Field on a Receipt 

D.1 Generation pipeline and prompt 

The E1 specifications reuse the round-v1 forgery catalogue (source image, target field, original and forged value, bound-ing box) and change only the generator [31, 32]. For each specification we expand the field bounding box by 50% per side (100-pixel floor) to form a context crop and snap it to the smallest legal output size, as in round v2: the endpoint requires both dimensions to be multiples of 16 and the pixel count to clear a floor, within an aspect ratio of [1:3 , 3:1] and a height of [128 , 2048] , so for a crop (Wc, H c) we take the smallest legal (W ∗, H ∗) with W ∗/H ∗ ≈ Wc/H c and down-sample the returned image back to (Wc, H c) with a Lanczos filter. The constraint made long receipts fail deterministically in round v2 (24.5% of the round-v1 ceiling failed), so this round draws only from crops satisfying it: the draw is a fil-ter, not a random sample. Round v2 could not pass a mask channel and drew a green outline for the prompt to treat as a marker; the direct API accepts a real alpha mask, so round v3 sends an opaque RGBA mask with the target field punched out to transparency and one fixed prompt, only the forged value substituted:                                         

> Edit only the transparent (masked) region of this document image. Replace the text there with exactly: "{forged_value}". Match the original font, size, weight, color, alignment, ink density and paper texture so the edit is indistinguishable from the rest of the document. Do not change anything outside the masked region. Output the full image.

This is shorter than round v2’s five-clause prompt [31], which had to explain a drawn rectangle. The template never varied, so no prompt-selection effect can inflate acceptance, though the shorter prompt is itself a confound in the round-v2 refusal comparison (§5.1). The returned crop is downsampled to the crop’s dimensions and pasted into the authentic document; pixels outside the crop are copied from the source and identical by construction. The ground-truth mask is the tight field box at source resolution, in the round-v1/v2 and DocTamper format [22]. 

Control cohort. The first 200 specifications, split evenly be-tween the two corpora, are also run on GPT-Image-2 through the same client, prompt and alpha mask in the same week, at 

low (196 tokens) and medium (1,756), since no GPT-Image-2 tier bills the 439 tokens of the Images 2.5 rows (400 attempts, 399 accepted, $7.88). 

D.2 Excluding inputs with mismatched sizes 

If the returned aspect ratio differs from the request’s by more than 2%, the blind resize distorts the pasted region and every pixel-level metric on it is meaningless. We flag such rows: 98 (4.9%) of document rows carry the flag, and the harness exporter refuses them. The exclusion is not ignorable. Flagged rows concentrate on WildReceipt (8.2% against 1.6%) and on long fields (7.7% against 1.7%), the most-flagged being 

Telephone_key (48) and Store_addr_key (22); they are 84% WildReceipt and 16% monetary against 48% and 48% among retained rows. Every E1 detector and locality number is therefore conditioned on a set that under-represents photographed receipts with long, wide fields. The E1 OCR analysis keeps flagged rows and reports the result without them as sensitivity (192 of 199 specifications). 

D.3 OCR checks and calibration 

OCR (easyocr) reads the target field and every other text line of the source crop and of each output. Target read (L1) compares the field’s OCR with the forged value, ignoring case, spacing and punctuation, and is scored only where OCR reads the source field correctly: 96 of 199 specifications, 62% on CORD but only 34% on WildReceipt photographs, which is why WildReceipt carries little of the L1 result. Any other text changed (L2) fires if a stable token differs when re-read in the output or new text appears. Each output is first registered to its source with the tolerant-locality registration and tokens are re-read at registered coordinates (738 stable tokens, against 601 in the unregistered analysis, which re-read at identical coordinates). Absolute rates depend on the token set, while the paired contrasts are robust to it (Appendix D.4). The OCR calibration rereads each source crop through noise replicas (a resampling round trip, a sub-pixel shift and a high-quality JPEG); a token is scored only if all replicas but one read it as the source does, and the held-out replica gives the rate at which OCR alone reports change: 12.1% for any other text under registration (10.0% CORD, 14.1% WildReceipt), against 15.6% at identical coordinates. Pairing controls the source specification but does not guarantee cancellation of measurement error. The calibration replicas are not matched to each arm’s shift distribution, so differential OCR error remains possible.

## E E2: Keep Editing the Same Image 

E2a procedure. Each of 60 receipts (30 CORD, 30 Wil-dReceipt) is edited 4 times by each model (GPT-Image-2 at 

medium and, added post hoc, low ), statelessly, one field per turn, with the unmasked instruction “change ‘ o’ to ‘ t’; keep everything else” in a seeded field order shared by all models. Targets keep the original’s character layout and are unique on the receipt; never-edited control fields are tracked too (213 planned fields on CORD, 217 on WildReceipt). After each turn OCR reads the receipt; an ORB and RANSAC similarity fit is used only to locate each field’s line, and there is no object detection or pixel-drift measure. Fields were pre-selected to be OCR-legible at t0, so the WildReceipt baseline (96%) is close to automatic and says nothing about OCR reliability on photographed receipts. ## I Localisation in Detail 

I.1 Localisation across our three dataset releases 

All three rounds forge the same specifications on the same source images, so a specification present in all three supports a comparison holding document, field, box and target value fixed while the generator changes, although the route and mask also changed between rounds. The intersection is 31 specifications, small because the round-v1 and round-v2 cells are staged in the harness as fixed subsets, and 30 of them carry a score in every arm. The localiser is DocTamper [22] at its released checkpoint ( dtd_doctamper ). Mean per-image localisation AUC is 0.728 [0.657, 0.798] on round v1 and 0.813 [0.753, 0.870] on round v2, and 0.595 [0.530, 0.658] on Flare and 0.553 [0.494, 0.616] on Sunburst in round v3 (Figure 5). On these 30 specifications the earlier rounds localise better than round v3, but the sample is small, the route and mask changed with the round, and the full-arm control of §5.2 shows GPT-Image-2 localised just as weakly through the round-v3 route. The panel is therefore consistent with weak localisation being a property of the round-v3 edit-ing route rather than of the newest models, and supports no stronger reading. Locality is not measurable on the staged round-v1 and round-v2 cells, which are re-encoded whole pages: a median 97% of their pixels differ from the source even outside the edited field.

I.3 Equivalence tests 

We test equivalence by two one-sided tests at the 5% level, that is, by whether a 90% cluster-bootstrap interval lies inside the margin. For DocTamper, each arm’s mean per-image local-isation AUC is tested against chance at margins of ±0.05 and 

±0.10, and each paired Images 2.5 − GPT-Image-2 difference against zero; the reported margin is the smallest the interval fits in. For Community Forensics, a model’s AUC counts as flat over its price tiers if every pairwise tier difference has a 90% interval inside ±0.03 or ±0.05 (an intersection-union test), resampling prompts and real images. Table 13 gives the results. No DocTamper arm lies within ±0.05 of chance, and 1 of 4 (Flare) lies within ±0.10. Flatness fails at ±0.03 for every model; at ±0.05 it holds only for GPT-Image-2’s 3 cells, and across all 13 1024-px cells the smallest supported margin is 0.080 (0.208 with the 4K cells). Localisation is therefore weak rather than absent, and detection shows no trend with price without being shown flat. 

## J Quality Settings, Cost, Speed and Prove-nance 

Design. The photoreal core is 100 text-to-image prompts over six content classes (portrait, street, interior, food, product and photographs of documents), written to be photographic rather than stylised. Every prompt runs at 1024 ×1024 on all three models at every tier that model offers, and a 20-prompt sub-grid adds 3840 ×2160 at high . Scored cells hold 30–100 images at 1024 px and 20 at 4K. Tokens are a price proxy, not a compute measure (§3.1). Table 14 is the grid behind §5.4. 

Detection across quality tiers. Community Forensics’ AUC against 544 real photographs stays within 0.88–0.94 across 13 1024-px cells spanning a 36-fold token range (Ta-ble 14). Part of every AUC may be format, since the real images are JPEG and TIFF. No trend test was run, and flatness is not established: equivalence at ±0.03 fails for every model, and the smallest margin supported across all 1024-px cells is 0.080. At 4K, billed at about 1.9 × the 1024 high price, AUC is lower for Flare (0.823 against 0.888) and Sunburst (0.775 against 0.917), on unmatched prompts. 

Attribution probe (exploratory). Images 2.5 ships as two models under one brand, so we asked whether pixels separate the three generators. A multinomial logistic regression on 128-d radial power-spectrum features, with standardised inputs under 5-fold stratified cross-validation on a balanced three-class split with prompt and tier held fixed, reaches 0.342 [0.267, 0.418] at medium (n = 152 ; chance 1/3), 0.395 [0.317, 0.472] at low , 0.474 [0.394, 0.553] at high and 0.595 [0.447, 0.744] in the 4K cells ( n = 42 ). Spectral features do not recover the generation at the tier most users get; a probe on detector embeddings would be the stronger test. 

Content credentials do not name the generation. All three models embed C2PA content credentials [4]. Reading the manifests back from our own raw PNGs, they declare the gen-erator as gpt-image version 2.0 for GPT-Image-2, Flare and Sunburst alike; that string is neither a model name nor our round v2. A defender reading content credentials therefore cannot distinguish an Images 2.5 image from a GPT-Image-2 one, and the platforms in the wild cross-check strip the manifests on upload (Appendix L).

## K Repeated Re-editing (“Laundering) in Detail 

A laundering chain repeatedly re-edits one generated image, as a forger would when reworking the same piece of evidence, so that detection can be read at several turn positions. The cell is complete: 1,200 randomised-order rows, 150 chains, all 150 of them complete eight-turn chains, with turns 1/3/5/8 scored by Community Forensics ( n = 50 per arm at every scored turn). Each of 50 seeds is a medium -tier photoreal image generated by the model that then edits it, and each goes through the same eight instruction edits on all three models: some local, some global edits of the whole frame, and a camera-look pass that is the degradation step a forger would apply. The order is a seeded random permutation per seed, identical across models, so that turn position and edit identity are separable; the camera-look edit lands at turn positions 1–8 with counts 5/4/7/10/4/5/10/5. An earlier fixed-order pilot of 30 rows is retired, and two control arms (an identity chain and a one-shot camera-look on the seed) were not run. Per-turn means with 95% intervals are plotted in Figure 6: GPT-Image-2 runs 0.24, 0.23, 0.28 and 0.34 at turns 1, 3, 5 and 8; Flare 0.15, 0.19, 0.17 and 0.18; and Sunburst 0.31, 0.27, 0.28 and 0.30. Broken out by edit identity, the camera-look pass ranks 8 of eight on GPT-Image-2 (0.12 against 0.23–0.35 for the other seven), 7 on Flare (0.10 against 0.10–0.30) and 8 on Sunburst (0.14 against 0.18–0.45). 

Does repeated editing move the detector? ## L Comparison with Images Posted Online 

We collected a new Images 2.5 sample using version-specific post filtering, extending the approach of our April GPT-Image-2 dataset [34]; that earlier paper does not document this new sample. The frozen release contains 1,382 standalone outputs, with posts dated 8 September 2026, 18:58:45 UTC through 10 September 2026, 02:00:30 UTC. Text must identify Im-ages 2.5 or Flare/Sunburst without also naming GPT-Image-2; comparison posts are excluded. Official X API rows addi-tionally pass a creation-language classifier, while gateway rows use the version gate alone. A vision-model filter re-tains standalone outputs. The detection result uses exactly 1,291 image IDs in the original native-precision score file; 91 release images have no score row and are outside this estimate. Membership rests on self-report. We freeze the scored subset as paper-b-wild-scored-20260911 ,with image IDs, timestamps, platform, file hashes and scores in anc/wild-scored-manifest.csv ; its SHA-256 is b730b595242dc0b2dd5884ccd1b1333b 5016cde655eff10c912dc25c4bf8bbd3 . These fixed IDs, rather than later companion-collection releases, define the reported sample. With no per-image authentic counterpart the wild number is a detection rate at a threshold calibrated on authentic images, not an AUC. On the path from our gen-erations to posted JPEGs, about 25.7 points of the gap lie between our generations and un-re-encoded posted PNGs and 8.0 between posted PNGs and posted JPEGs. The PNG subset is not content-matched (the one source that preserves PNG is illustration-heavy), so this describes the gap rather than attributing it. Pooled over images rather than cells, the con-trolled rate is 67.6% of 860 (Wilson 95% 64.4%–70.6%). The gap bears on whether the detection numbers transfer to im-ages posted online, not on the validity of the capability and review-check measurements. 

## M Limitations 

Scope, and the five limitations that bear on how these results are used. Our results cover four forgery tasks, two Images 2.5 models and GPT-Image-2 at two quality tiers. “Localisation” refers to DocTamper on masked receipt edits, “detection” to Community Forensics on whole images, and no test involves faces. First, no human rated the outputs. An AI assistant (Claude) spot-checked samples of the automatic scores, but not the aligned OCR comparison we now report, and the automatic checks themselves make errors; human replication is future Evaluating ChatGPT Images 2.5 on Forgery Tasks 25 

work. Second, each E1 edit is made on a context crop that is pasted back into the page. This leaves a seam that TruFor picks up and that may affect DocTamper, so our localisation results apply to crop-and-paste editing only: a forger who edits the whole page leaves no such seam (Appendix I.2). Third, the inputs are partly synthetic or pre-selected. The product images are our own renders, so fidelity on photographed products is untested, and OCR reads few WildReceipt fields reliably, so the reduction in unintended changes and Flare’s weaker repeated-editing result both rest mainly on the CORD receipts. Fourth, all calls came from one account in one week through the API rather than the ChatGPT interface, each input was sampled once because the API cannot be seeded, and parts of the analysis plan were fixed after results were known. Where we report no difference, in refusal between gener-ations and in detection across quality tiers, we failed to find one; we did not show the two are equal. Fifth, the fine-print test measures only the size range above the OCR limit, a range with relatively high, but imperfect, exact-read rates, so its null means no measurable difference there rather than equal fine-print fidelity. That range is not saturated and one contrast does separate at matched glyph height, so we treat the sharper-detail claim as unsettled rather than refuted. The rest of this section gives the narrower limitations and the measurements behind them; Appendix G plots the legibility curve by glyph height. 

The refusal comparison, axis by axis. Rounds v2 and v3 differ in generator, route, mask mechanism, prompt and cor-pus set at once. The control fixes all but the generation within round v3 and closes nothing else in the round-v2 compari-son. Two explanations stand: the round-v3 request elicits compliance where round v2’s drawn box did not, or classi-fier behaviour on this misuse class relaxed between April and September for every model served; being contemporaneous, the control moves with the 2.5 arms under the second. The arm that would separate them, Images 2.5 in round v2’s drawn-box format, was not run. At 400 attempts against 2,000 the control’s interval is the widest of the three, so the rates are of the same order, not shown to be equal (Table 12). 

The descoped judge panel and the preliminary image-level number. Round v2’s grid (non-expert humans, TruFor, Doc-Tamper and the generator as self-judge) was descoped for round v3. A preliminary image-level DocTamper number exists (0.588 [0.544, 0.633] Flare, 0.610 [0.567, 0.652] Sun-burst, against 200 authentic Images 2.5 document pages), but it aggregates each pixel map by the 99th percentile of its pixel scores rather than by round v2’s rule, so it does not belong beside round v2’s 0.585. Whether round-v3 forgeries are detected more or less often than round v2’s is open. 

The narrower limitations, in full. 

• No human rating. Every visual check is a spot check of a scorer by an AI coding agent (Claude), and none covers the registered E1 token set; OCR, OWLv2 and the homography are machine checkers with their own error, read against floors and ceilings. • OCR limits. Capability and review-check rates are OCR reads, compared with an aligned calibration false-change rate (E1) or a smallest-readable size (E3, E4). OCR cannot read text below about 9 px, and OCR frequently fails below that size, so E4 cannot tell whether Images 2.5 renders smaller text better; its null result says more about OCR than about the models, and the models contribute different readable lines. • WildReceipt pre-selection and E1 selection. OCR reads 34% of E1’s WildReceipt source fields, and E2a fields were chosen to be OCR-legible at t0, so E1’s reduction in unin-tended changes and Flare’s weaker E2a result rest on the CORD receipts. E1 also samples the catalogue through an aspect-ratio filter, conditioned on geometry both before and after generation, and the second filter is not ignorable (Appendix D.2). • Synthetic references. E3 uses flat programmatic packshots; the completed rendered set is an exploratory check, and people are not tested. • When the analysis plan was fixed. The primary contrasts for E1, E3 and E4, and the statistics framework, were fixed with results known, and E1 re-scores images generated before its design document. The E2 rules were fixed before any full-run E2a estimate, but after the chat-mode E2b data had been scored in full (hence exploratory) and after a pilot run had shown Flare’s numbers. Only the low -setting addendum preceded its data (Appendix C.1). • Post hoc controls and analyses. The GPT-Image-2 low 

arms of E2a, E2b and E3, and every post hoc analysis listed in Appendix C.1, were added after the main results were known. • Thresholds after a pilot. E2b’s umbrella colour share was revised after a pilot miss; it affects only the exploratory conversational contrasts (Appendix E). • Unequal price. No GPT-Image-2 tier bills 439 tokens: low 

(196) costs less and medium (1,756) four times as much, so neither contrast is at equal price. The low runs of E2a, E2b and E3 ran about 11 hours after the configurations they are compared with. • Batch and synchronous rows. Routes are mixed within E2– E4, unevenly by model in stateless E2b (Table 3), and batch rows carry no latency. Latency uses synchronous rows from one account in serial, not concurrent throughput, and the E1 configurations were not interleaved. • API, not the ChatGPT interface. Product behaviour is not measured, and E2b’s chat variant relays instructions through 

gpt-5.4-nano , which can rewrite placement. • Equivalence margins. No model’s DocTamper localisa-tion is equivalent to chance within ±0.05, and Community Forensics flatness across tiers fails at ±0.03. • TruFor coverage. TruFor scored only pages up to about 2,772 px on the long edge (7.4%–8.3% of pages unscored per configuration), so its numbers are conditioned on page size.
